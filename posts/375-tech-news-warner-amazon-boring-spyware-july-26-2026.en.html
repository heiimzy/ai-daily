⚡ July 26 Tech Roundup: Warner Bros Sues Amazon Over 'Lawless Employee Shopping Spree,' Musk's Boring Company Targets $20B Valuation, the Spyware-Hunting Hacker Who Vanished

1. Warner Bros Discovery Sues Amazon for Poaching Executives

Warner Bros Discovery (WBD) filed a lawsuit this week in California Superior Court accusing Amazon of illegally poaching contracted executives, calling it a "lawless employee shopping spree."

The lawsuit centers on Pia Barlow, former Senior Vice President of Originals Marketing at HBO Max. According to WBD, Barlow's employment contract ran until October 31, 2027, but she notified the company of her intent to resign on May 26 and formally resigned on June 5. She then accepted a position at Amazon MGM Studios as Head of Original Series Marketing, starting August 3.

WBD alleges that Amazon, fully aware of Barlow's existing contract, "brazenly and deliberately induced Barlow to breach the employment agreement" by offering a higher compensation package and promising to cover her legal defense costs if sued for breach of contract. The lawsuit states: "Amazon recognized that Barlow's loyalty was for sale."

According to the complaint, this was not Amazon's first attempt. Just weeks earlier, Amazon had allegedly tried to poach another WBD executive — believed to be HBO's Head of Drama Series and Films, Francesca Orsi — though that attempt was unsuccessful. The lawsuit warns that additional poaching efforts are ongoing.

WBD is seeking preliminary and permanent injunctions preventing Amazon from inducing contract breaches, plus unspecified monetary damages. The case is expected to reignite debate over the enforceability of fixed-term employment agreements under California law, particularly as tech companies — which rarely use such contracts — increasingly poach talent from traditional media companies.

"Rather than build its entertainment-production workforce from the ground up, Amazon has chosen to ride on the coattails of other well-established Hollywood mainstays by inducing contracted employees to breach their employment agreements." — Warner Bros Discovery complaint

2. Musk's Boring Company Seeks $4B Funding at $20B Valuation

Elon Musk's tunneling startup The Boring Company is in talks to raise approximately $4 billion in new funding at a valuation of about $20 billion, according to The Wall Street Journal. If completed, the valuation would more than triple the company's $5.7 billion valuation following its 2022 funding round.

Founded in 2016, The Boring Company develops tunneling technology and transportation systems designed to carry passenger pods through underground, low-pressure tubes between cities. Its most prominent project to date is the Vegas Loop beneath the Las Vegas Convention Center, which has transported over 3 million passengers since opening in 2021. New projects are advancing in Nashville and Dubai.

The company raised $675 million in its previous round in 2022 from investors including Vy Capital, Sequoia Capital, and Founders Fund, bringing total capital raised to approximately $908 million. Notably, this fundraising push comes despite sharp stock declines at Musk's other ventures, Tesla and SpaceX, suggesting private-market enthusiasm for Musk-affiliated companies remains strong.

Sources cautioned that the deal has not yet closed and terms could change. If the round closes at the target valuation and size, it would rank among the largest private technology fundraising deals in recent years.

3. The Hacker Who Humiliated Spyware Makers and Was Never Caught

A decade after his most famous hack, the mysterious figure known as Phineas Fisher remains, by most accounts, the most prolific and public hacker never to have been caught. TechCrunch published a deep dive into this cybersecurity legend's story.

Phineas Fisher first emerged in August 2014, announcing they had hacked Gamma Group, the German makers of the FinFisher spyware. They publicized the breach via a cheeky Twitter account called @GammaGroupPR, leaking stolen data including mobile spyware source code, product manuals, and price lists. After publishing a post-mortem doubling as a leftist manifesto, they vanished.

A year later, they returned with a bang, hacking Hacking Team, an Italian startup that was among the first to turn government spyware into a viable global business — paving the way for companies like Israel's NSO Group. They extracted over 400 gigabytes of data: source code, tens of thousands of internal emails, confidential contracts, and customer lists. The leak enabled journalists to expose scandals in Ecuador, Mexico, and Panama. Years later, Hacking Team CEO David Vincenzetti was forced to sell the company for one euro. For former employees, Phineas's hack marked the beginning of the end.

That was their last public appearance. Their Twitter and Reddit accounts have long been deleted, leaving no online trail. The Italian investigation into the Hacking Team hack concluded without finding evidence pointing to Phineas's real identity. According to TechCrunch reporter Lorenzo Franceschi-Bicchierai, who has maintained contact, Phineas is alive and well and communicated within the last couple of years.

"Phineas remains, by most measures, the most prolific and public hacker never to have been caught." — TechCrunch
