⚡ July 26 International Politics: Kazakhstan's President Tells Putin to "Freeze the War" to His Face, Russia Abandons 20-Year Korean Denuclearization Consensus, 30,000 More North Korean Troops Headed to Ukraine, US Studies Plan to Seize Iran's Enriched Uranium

July 26 marks a day of significant geopolitical shifts across multiple fronts. In a stunning public intervention, Kazakhstan President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev — one of Moscow's closest allies — directly urged Vladimir Putin to freeze the war in Ukraine during a live televised meeting in Siberia. In Asia, Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov announced at the ASEAN ministerial meeting that Moscow would no longer support calls for Korean Peninsula denuclearization, upending a two-decade international consensus. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy revealed that Russia is preparing to receive 30,000 additional North Korean troops, while U.S. media reported the Pentagon is actively studying a plan to deploy thousands of soldiers to seize Iran's enriched uranium stockpile. Here is the full breakdown.

1. Kazakhstan's Tokayev Urges Putin to Freeze the Ukraine War — A Rare Public Rebuke

Speaking at the 22nd Russia-Kazakhstan Interregional Cooperation Forum in Omsk, Siberia on Saturday, President Tokayev delivered a remarkable appeal directly to Putin, urging him to freeze the conflict and return to the Istanbul negotiation framework first explored in the early weeks of the war.

"Perhaps it's time to freeze this conflict and return to the Istanbul formula 2.0, since significant results were achieved there," Tokayev said in remarks broadcast live on Russian television. "This requires guarantees from major powers, including Russia, to then proceed from there."

The Kazakh leader added that he receives "numerous signals from Europe and the U.S. regarding the state of the conflict," and that people had asked him to relay these messages. He went further, expressing personal confusion about the war's rationale: "The essence of this conflict is not entirely clear to many — including ourselves. Unlike the Armenia-Azerbaijan conflict, whose historical roots are very clear, this situation is hard to grasp."

Putin responded by saying he would brief Tokayev on the "details" of the war and arrange for Russian officials to provide relevant information. Notably, the Russian president did not dismiss Tokayev's proposal outright, a subtle shift from Moscow's usual categorical rejection of ceasefire calls.

Kazakhstan has been careful not to publicly criticize Russia's military operation since its launch in 2022, making Tokayev's televised intervention all the more significant. The rare public nudge from one of Russia's closest partners signals growing unease among Moscow's allies about the protracted conflict's economic and geopolitical toll.

Ukraine Strikes Deep into Russian Territory Simultaneously

On the same day, Ukrainian forces continued long-range strikes deep inside Russia. Zelenskyy confirmed the successful attack on the Tyumen oil refinery in western Siberia — over 2,000 kilometers from the Ukrainian border. The Russian Athletics Federation announced that the All-Russian athletics competition in Yekaterinburg was suspended due to drone threats. Russia's Defense Ministry reported that over 300 Ukrainian drones were launched against Russian territory on Saturday, covering regions including Siberia.

2. Russia Abandons Korean Peninsula Denuclearization Stance — Breaking 22-Year Consensus

In a press conference following the ASEAN Foreign Ministers' Meeting, Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov dropped a diplomatic bombshell: Moscow will no longer support any international calls for the denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula.

Lavrov argued that North Korea's nuclear development is an act of self-defense by a sovereign state under "extreme pressure," pushed to the corner by joint U.S.-Japan-South Korea military pressure. He pointed out that the 2005 Six-Party Talks Joint Statement called for denuclearization of the "entire Korean Peninsula," but Washington, Tokyo, and Seoul now only demand Pyongyang give up its weapons while ignoring the U.S. nuclear umbrella deployed in South Korea and Japan.

This effectively overturns a policy red line Moscow maintained for 22 years since the Beijing Six-Party Talks began in 2003. Regardless of how U.S.-Russia relations deteriorated, Moscow never wavered on the principle of "denuclearization" during those two decades — a consensus that underpinned international coordination on North Korea.

Ripple Effects: East Asia's Nuclear Taboo Under Threat

  • For Pyongyang: A lifeline: With a permanent UN Security Council member and nuclear power publicly backing its position, any diplomatic push for North Korean denuclearization is effectively dead. Pyongyang now has Moscow as an alternative channel, reducing Beijing's unique mediating role.

Lavrov revealed that North Korean Foreign Minister Choe Son-hui was personally briefed on this policy shift by Putin during her visit to Moscow last week, confirming the decision was carefully calibrated at the highest level before being publicly announced.

3. Zelenskyy: Russia Preparing to Receive 30,000 More North Korean Troops

In his nightly address on Saturday, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy disclosed that Russia is actively preparing to receive up to 30,000 additional North Korean soldiers to replenish its battlefield losses.

"Russia wants to receive another 30,000 troops from North Korea. Since June, preparations have been underway in Russia's Voronezh region to receive them," Zelenskyy stated. He also warned that North Korea is preparing to transfer additional ballistic missile launchers to Russia.

North Korea is estimated to have already sent approximately 20,000 combat troops and military engineers to Russia's Kursk region, with around 14,000 currently deployed on front lines. If the additional 30,000 arrive, total North Korean forces deployed in Russia would exceed 40,000 — the largest foreign military deployment on Russian soil since World War II.

Zelenskyy framed the Russia-North Korea partnership as a global security threat: "Russia is helping North Korea learn how to wage war, improve its weapons, and gain real combat experience in using them. All of this is a threat to everyone in Asia who is within range of North Korean missiles."

4. Pentagon Reportedly Studies Plan for Thousands of Troops to Seize Iran's Enriched Uranium

The New York Post reported Saturday, citing informed sources, that the U.S. government is actively studying an extremely sensitive military option — seizing Iran's enriched uranium stockpile from its nuclear facilities, potentially requiring thousands of troops.

The operation is described as "extremely complex," potentially requiring thousands of Army soldiers to assault nuclear facilities, breach minefields, and deploy engineering teams to secure perimeter defenses. A small special operations team would then execute the core uranium extraction — a process described as "extremely dangerous." Throughout the operation, U.S. forces would need to clear debris-blocked facility entrances, repel Iranian resistance, and maintain full control of air corridors for safe extraction.

Iranian military sources told Sputnik that Iran's armed forces have rehearsed various U.S. attack scenarios and are fully prepared for a ground invasion. Analysts note that the disclosure of this planning reflects escalating Washington anxiety about Iran's nuclear trajectory — particularly under Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, who succeeded his father in March and is assessed by U.S. intelligence as having a "far higher" willingness to pursue nuclear weapons than his late father.

5. IRGC Warns UK: You Could Become a Target If You Support the US

Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) issued a new warning on Saturday: if Britain expands its support for the United States, the UK itself could become a military target.

An IRGC spokesperson stated that any country supporting the U.S., including Britain and Gulf states, would be considered a "legitimate military target" if it assists U.S. operations. The spokesperson specifically noted that U.S. bombers have recently used British airfields, calling the UK's neutrality into question.

The UK government had previously confirmed the deployment of additional military assets to the Middle East to support regional maritime security. This latest warning from Iran means the conflict's "geographic radiation zone" is systematically expanding — from the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, from the Persian Gulf to the Caspian, from the Black Sea to the Baltic — European countries increasingly find themselves pushed to the front lines of risk without having been directly involved.

"None of us in the world should turn a blind eye to one very simple fact: evil always seeks ways to make things worse and spread further. Russia must be stopped. This war must be stopped. Pressure on the aggressor must work." — President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, July 25 address

As of late July 26, the U.S.-Iran relationship sits at a delicate crossroads. After 13 consecutive nights of airstrikes, Iran experienced its first quiet night without American bombing. Trump has set aside plans for major escalation, and Omani mediation in Tehran is reportedly making progress — Axios cited sources saying talks are proceeding well and a deal could be reached this weekend. Yet the parallel stories of Russia abandoning denuclearization, North Korea surging troops into Ukraine, and the U.S. weighing a nuclear seizure operation in Iran reveal a deeper trend: the global great-power chessboard is being reshuffled at an accelerating pace, and the post-Cold War security architecture faces systemic strain on multiple fronts simultaneously.
