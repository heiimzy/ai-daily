⚡ Aug 3 Finance Update: US & Japan Rarely Team Up to Lift the Yen, Fed's 9-3 Vote Exposes a Rare Split, Shanghai Index Back Above 4,000

On Monday afternoon, global financial markets revolved around two themes: currencies and trust. Tokyo and Washington unusually joined hands to buy the yen, yanking it sharply off a 40-year low; meanwhile, the Federal Reserve's rate decision delivered the rarest run of hawkish dissents since 2016, and the 30-year Treasury yield raced to around a two-decade high as faith in the central bank's guidance eroded. And in China, A-shares closed firmly higher, pushing the Shanghai Index back above the 4,000-point mark while Cambricon briefly reclaimed the crown from its peers. Here is this roundup of global finance news.

1. US-Japan Joint FX Intervention: Yen Jumps 3.8% in Two Sessions, Tokyo Possibly Spends ~$59B

On Monday, Japan's Finance Ministry and the U.S. Treasury almost simultaneously confirmed a rare coordinated intervention — jointly buying the yen to prop up the currency. The previous day, U.S. President Donald Trump told reporters on Air Force One that Washington was acting as "a gesture of friendship" and expects financial benefit from the move. Japanese Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama is set to formally announce the measures on Monday.

Markets reacted violently: the yen rose as much as 1.4% to touch a three-month high near 155.20 per dollar, compounding a surge of 3.8% over two sessions; the dollar index lost ground, the euro climbed to a six-week high of $1.1559, and sterling approached two-week highs. According to Bank of Japan data, Tokyo may have sold up to about $58.97 billion to buy yen during Thursday's New York session. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Washington "will not hesitate" to take part in further joint action and, along with Tokyo, again called for the Bank of Japan to hike rates. Analysts point out that if the intervention trend continues, the long-running yen carry trade that relies on low-cost yen funding could reach a turning point — some even compare it to a precursor of a "new Plaza Accord." Notably, the Bank of Korea also stepped in to buy the won on Thursday, as regional currency-coordination efforts take shape.

2. Fed Vote 9-3 to Hold: Three Hawkish Dissents, the Most Since 2016

At its July 29 meeting, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) voted 9-3 to hold the policy rate at 3.50%-3.75%, keeping it unchanged for a fifth straight meeting. But the headline was the dissent: Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan and Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari all voted to raise rates by 25 basis points. It is the first time since September 2016 that three members dissented in the same direction; for new Chair Kevin Warsh, facing three dissents so early in his term is a "first in 56 years." U.S. 12-month inflation reached a three-year high of 4.2% in May.

With Warsh declining to offer clear forward guidance, bond markets did the tightening themselves. The 30-year Treasury yield at one point rose above 5.26%, near the highest since 2007, while the 10-year yield climbed above 4.7%. Surging long-dated yields amount to de facto financial tightening: if the Fed eventually hikes to stabilize prices, it could slow the AI-infrastructure-driven bull market that has underpinned stocks — leaving equities in a lose-lose position if the Fed sits still, and also if it acts.

3. 'Bond Market Storm' Worries: Zandi Warns of a Serious Sell-Off

Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics, warned this weekend that the bigger threat is the Fed's reluctance to offer "even a modicum of forward guidance." He wrote that "Suddenly, there is a new potential threat to the economy — a serious mistake by the Federal Reserve," adding that if the opacity persists, a future meeting could trigger a serious market sell-off that spills into the real economy. Economists criticized Warsh's communications style as "less guidance, less explanation, and a lot more uncertainty," while former Fed economist Claudia Sahm said the statement failed to reflect recent employment and inflation trends. Traders are also piling into options: heavy positioning targets lifting the 10-year yield to near 4.9% by August 21 (potentially the highest since 2023), while others see the 30-year breaking above 5.4% — effectively letting bond yields "do the Fed's tightening."

4. A-Share Close: Shanghai Back Above 4,000, Cambricon's Market Cap Again Tops That of Key AI Names

A-shares opened higher and kept climbing on Monday. The Shanghai Composite closed up 0.97% at 4,007.76 points, back above the 4,000 mark; the Shenzhen Component Index rose 1.73% to 13,452.42; the ChiNext jumped 1.84% to 3,224.62; and the STAR 50 index gained over 3%. Combined turnover reached about RMB 2.06 trillion (¥930.3B in Shanghai, ¥1,125B in Shenzhen), up meaningfully from the prior session; 2,880 advancers beat 2,388 decliners, with 72 limit-ups and 21 limit-downs.

Chips and AI computing power led the rally: after "AMD's AI chips have received an export license to China" surfaced in a conference call, combined with a stronger "domestic chips substitution" narrative, expectations around domestic compute rose. Cambricon surged to briefly push its market cap back above that of Moutai and others, while Haiguang Information also jumped, lifting the STAR 50 index more than 3%. Main-fund flows concentrated in semiconductors, components and auto parts; phosphorous chemicals (Qingshuiyuan limit-up 20%), aluminum (Nanshan Aluminium hit limit-up), controllable fusion and humanoid-robot themes also firmed in the afternoon. Analysts broadly expect advanced chips and technology growth to remain a key allocation direction over the medium to long term, even if AI capital-expenditure chips in uncertainty in the near term.
