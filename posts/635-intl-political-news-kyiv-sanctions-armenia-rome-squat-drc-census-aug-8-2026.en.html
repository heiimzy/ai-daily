⚡ Aug 8 World Affairs: Russian Missile Strikes Near Kyiv Kill Three Including a Child, US Senate Passes New Russia Sanctions, Armenia Trials Church Leader, Rome Squat Crisis & DRC's First Census in 42 Years

Within the past hour or two, several geopolitical and domestic fronts have developed at once across Europe, the Middle East and Africa: Russian missiles again struck Ukraine's capital and its environs, killing three people including a child; the US Senate passed a new round of Russia energy sanctions by 86-11; Armenia began trying its Apostolic Church leader and six senior clergy over alleged spying for Russia; in Rome, a building squatted for decades was sealed and about 300 residents were forced onto the scorching street in temperatures above 40°C; Palestinian Christians are being pushed out of their homeland by violence; and the Democratic Republic of Congo launched its first national census in nearly 42 years. Here is the detailed, timestamp-verified breakdown.

1. Russia Strikes Near Kyiv: 3 Killed, Including a Child, as Air-Defence Gap Widens

Early on Saturday, air-raid sirens sounded over Kyiv. Ukrainian officials confirmed Russian missiles struck three locations in the Boryspil district just outside the capital, killing three people — one of them a child — and wounding three more; two districts of the capital caught fire, and residents in central Kyiv reported powerful explosions shaking nearby buildings. The details were reported by the head of the regional administration, Tymur Tkachenko.

Analysts note that Russian ballistic missiles travel at several times the speed of sound and dive almost vertically, leaving defenders only seconds to react. At present only US-supplied Patriot systems can reliably intercept them, and Kyiv says it lacks anywhere near enough batteries and interceptors to shield its cities. This week President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Ukraine had received only about a third of as many air-defence missiles in the first half of this year as in the same period of 2025, blaming the shortfall on the US–Israel war on Iran draining global stocks. Ukrainian government advisers estimate Russia can now launch about 100 ballistic missiles a month and is preparing to escalate further ahead of winter.

In parallel, Ukraine kept up deep strikes: within the last 24 hours it hit two oil refineries inside Russia and launched long-range drones at an e-commerce warehouse of retailer Wildberries in the city of Yekaterinburg. Moscow, for its part, accused Ukrainian drones of hitting an apartment block in Crimea and killing two people.

2. US Senate Passes New Russia Sanctions: 86-11, Targeting Oil and Gas Exports

Almost in sync with the strikes, the US Senate on Friday (by Beijing time Saturday) passed, 86-11, a sweeping new sanctions bill targeting Russian energy exports — including a 100% tariff on countries still buying Russian oil and gas. The legislation is named after the late US Senator Lindsey Graham, a longtime champion of tougher measures against Russia, and is designed to drain Russian energy revenue and raise the cost to foreign governments that keep buying it.

Analysts say the bill, layered on top of the Trump administration's existing pressure, pushes the West's sanctions framework toward a new "energy map" phase — the next question is whether China and India, Moscow's two biggest energy buyers, will adjust their procurement routes. That is part of why the move is seen by some as a measure that "could hurt India and China."

3. Armenia Puts Its Church Leader on Trial: Alleged Spying for Russia

In the South Caucasus, Armenia's Apostolic Church leader and six senior clergy have gone on trial accused of spying for Russia. Authorities indicate the seven could face up to two years in prison. A country where the Orthodox and other traditions run deep, Armenia has seen persistent tension between the church and the government of Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan.

Critics call the trial clearly politically motivated — part of a wider crackdown on Pashinyan's opponents; the government describes it as "lawfully countering infiltration by foreign powers." The case exposes Armenia's deep fracture between "de-Russification," pro-European aspirations and domestic governance, especially sensitive since the 2023 defeat in Nagorno-Karabakh reset the country's geopolitical standing.

4. Rome Squat Crisis: Sealed Building, about 300 People Sleep in the Heat

In Rome, Italy, the seven-storey former office block known as "Spin Time" was emptied and then judicially sealed after a small fire on the evening of 29 July in an unoccupied basement room. Squat-taken since 2013, the building was home to many people from over 20 nationalities (about 140 families were built communities) and was one of Rome's most active cultural spaces — with a free co-working area, a newspaper made by people under 30, and concerts and plays.

Now about 300 people, including roughly 100 children, are camping on the street in sustained temperatures above 40°C. Many residents have not slept for tens of hours, queuing by the building waiting to be escorted by police one at a time to collect personal documents and essentials; volunteers deliver food, water and mattresses at midday. Dozens of politicians and public figures have shown up in support. One woman who lived in the building for nearly 11 years said her brother is blind and in a wheelchair, previously looked after by neighbours while she worked; now he sits under the scorching sun. "Inequality and marginalisation are the termites eating away at our democracy."

In 2019 the building made global headlines when the pope's almoner, Cardinal Konrad Krajewski, descended into a manhole to restore residents' electricity, cut because of mounting debts. Now the fight over "housing as a fundamental right" is back on Rome's streets, echoing Italy's recent debate over tightening borders amid the Ceuta migrant influx.

5. Palestinian Christians Leaving the "Holy Land" Under Violence; DRC starts Its First Census in 42 Years

In the Middle East, Palestinian Christians are being driven out of their ancestral land by violence and displacement across Gaza, Jerusalem and the occupied West Bank, with attacks even targeting churches and clergy shrinking an already small community. Footage shows believers displaced from the Church of the Holy Sepulchre to regional parishes — a painful chapter of a wider war and a sign of this multi-faith region's fragile social fabric.

In Africa, the DRC has launched its first national census since 1984 (when it was still called Zaire), nearly 42 years. The World Bank puts the population at around 112 million with annual growth of roughly 3.2%; yet millions of Congolese have never been officially registered, with only voter cards serving as limited identification. The census will begin with digital mapping of inhabited areas, then household data collection. For many it means "finally going to count as a citizen" — a 28-year-old in Bunia, Ituri province, put it plainly: "I've never been counted." (via Al Jazeera)
