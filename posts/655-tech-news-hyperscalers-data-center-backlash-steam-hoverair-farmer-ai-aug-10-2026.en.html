⚡ Aug 10 Tech Update: Hyperscalers Top $2T in Commitments, 70% of Americans Oppose Data Centers, Steam's Europe Distributor Hit by Cyberattack, HoverAir Unveils Modular Drone-Camera, AI Farming Advice Wipes Out Crops

A heavy Monday-evening dump of general tech news: the four big hyperscalers' long-term purchase commitments now approach $2 trillion, pushing memory from commodity to strategic asset; a poll finds over 70% of Americans oppose data centers near their homes, and a $700 million Nashville project beside a zoo was blocked; Steam's European hardware distributor CEVA suffered a cyberattack, prompting Valve to warn buyers about phishing; HoverAir unveiled the Versa, a pocket gimbal camera that turns into an AI-tracking drone; and a stark cautionary tale of a 67-year-old Chinese farmer who lost 24.7 acres of sesame after following AI pesticide advice. All source publication times were verified within the two-hour window.

1. Hyperscalers' Commitments Near $2 Trillion: Memory Becomes a Strategic Asset

UK tech outlet Tom's Hardware (published Aug 10, 12:00 GMT), drawing on analyst Claus Aasholm's estimates, reports that the combined long-term purchase commitments of Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Microsoft reached nearly $2 trillion (about $1.968T) by Q2 2026, with Alphabet and Microsoft accounting for the overwhelming majority (Source: Tom's Hardware).

The growth is highly uneven: Google (Alphabet) is by far the most aggressive, surging from roughly $140-150 billion in Q3 2025 to about $811 billion by Q2 2026; Microsoft follows a similar path to about $678 billion in total obligations; Meta has ramped to roughly $349.3 billion; and Amazon grew more gradually to about $130 billion. By contrast, Apple — once the largest buyer of memory — has stayed almost flat at roughly $57 billion, of which $56.2 billion falls due within 12 months; Nvidia's own commitments stand at about $119 billion.

The report stresses these are total purchase obligations, spanning foundry capacity, 3D NAND, DRAM and EMS manufacturing — not memory purchases alone. Still, the direction is unmistakable: memory is being repositioned from a shelf commodity into a "competitive weapon", giving memory makers like Micron, Samsung and SK hynix greater pricing power and expansion incentives, while hyperscalers — not traditional consumer-electronics firms — increasingly call the shots.

2. Over 70% of Americans Oppose Data Centers; Nashville Zoo Project Blocked

Also per Tom's Hardware (published Aug 10, 11:40 GMT), the grassroots backlash against AI data centers keeps intensifying (Source: Tom's Hardware). In the latest move, Nashville residents and local politicians moved to block a $700 million data center slated for right next to the Nashville Zoo at Grassmere — the city council approved a blocking measure last week, and Mayor Freddie O'Connell will use it to stop the project. Earlier, a Kentucky farming family turned down an offer worth 10 times their land's value.

Polling shows more than 70% of Americans now don't want data centers near their homes — making them less popular than nuclear power plants. Pushback extends to the regulatory level: Virginia's State Corporation Commission just ruled that data centers must pay for the high-voltage transmission infrastructure they exclusively use, easing residents' energy bills. In the emblematic Colossus case, Musk's Texas AI factory was found to be using permitless mobile gas turbines; SpaceX has now agreed to remove all roughly 69 of them (a move taking over a year).

Over one July weekend alone, opponents staged 142 protests across 42 states; around 37-40 people have been arrested so far in 2026 in connection with data-center protests, most for violating "petty rules." Close to 70 jurisdictions around the country have now placed moratoriums on — or permanently blocked — new data center builds.

3. Steam's Europe Distributor CEVA Hit by Cyberattack; Valve Warns of Phishing

Per Tom's Hardware (published Aug 10, 11:49 GMT), CEVA, Steam's German hardware distributor, was hit by a cyberattack between July 29 and August 1, 2026; Valve learned on August 7 that hackers had swiped information on Steam hardware customers (Source: Tom's Hardware).

The leaked data is delivery-related (CEVA retains it for 90 days to fulfill orders) and likely covers names, addresses, phone numbers, emails, and the type and price of products ordered. Valve stresses that payment information, passwords and Steam Guard codes were not exposed — which is precisely why attackers may use the stolen details for targeted phishing.

Valve's public warning: anyone who bought Steam hardware (e.g. Steam Deck, Steam Machine) and had it fulfilled in Europe in recent months should "expect fake messages — email, SMS or phone" that mention their hardware order and appear to come from Steam, Valve or a delivery company. Common angles include requesting extra customs or delivery fees, or steering victims to fake log-in portals.Ignore such requests. Steam log-ins occur only on help.steampowered.com, store.steampowered.com, www.steampowered.com and steamcommunity.com, and "Steam Support will never ask you for your password or a Steam Guard code. Neither will a courier." CEVA has isolated the affected systems, notified data-protection authorities and brought in outside investigators.

4. HoverAir Unveils Versa: A Pocket Gimbal Camera That Becomes an AI-Tracking Drone

Per Tom's Hardware (published Aug 10, 11:20 GMT), HoverAir has unveiled the Versa, its first pocket camera — and its party trick is that it can transform into an AI-follow drone (Source: Tom's Hardware).

As a camera, the Versa packs a three-axis gimbal (most pocket cameras offer only two-axis stabilization), a rotating screen, dedicated controls and RGB accent lighting that doubles as a status indicator. Snap a small foldable drone body to the back via magnetic pogo pins, unfold the wings, and a light tap launches it straight from your palm.

  • No controller needed: the device uses AI to track and follow you automatically, with 10+ intelligent modes and AI-assisted framing.
  • 360° "3D Worlds": the drone flies around to snap the scene from different angles and composites a shareable 360° view.
  • Specs TBD: no price or release date yet, but HoverAir touts class-leading dynamic range; based on prior models, expect up to 8K 10-bit Log/HLG video and flight speeds up to ~45 km/h.

The outlet notes the "magnetic combo" concept is unlike anything on the market — a little "magical" — and that if HoverAir can fuse its excellent X1-series selfie drones with a solid pocket camera, the Versa could carve out a brand-new category.

5. 67-Year-Old Chinese Farmer Loses 24.7 Acres of Sesame After Following AI Advice

Per Tom's Hardware (published Aug 10, 10:00 GMT, citing Taiwanese outlet CTWANT), a 67-year-old farmer surnamed Wu in Chuzhou, China, who had been using AI for about a year, followed an AI-generated weed-and-pest plan that killed roughly 150 mu (about 24.7 acres) of sesame seedlings overnight (Source: Tom's Hardware).

Wu initially distrusted the AI app but gradually came to trust it after it repeatedly gave useful advice. This time, the AI generated a "Hundred Acres of Sesame Grass Control + Pest Control" plan recommending high-efficiency flupyrimethalin and flusulfasulfaether for weeds, mixed with thiamethoxazine and methyl salt. Wu followed it to the letter without checking with agricultural technicians or counter-searching online — and by the next day the weeds and sesame seedlings died en masse.

Pressed for a cause, the AI blamed the "flusulfasulfaether." Agricultural experts explain that flusulfasulfaether primarily targets broadleaf weeds in soybean fields — and the USDA classifies sesame as a broadleaf plant, so it is easily harmed too; the chemical is also designed for spot, targeted application, not whole-field spraying. Ironic as it sounds, the AI chat page carried a reminder reading: "AI generation may be incorrect, please verify." The report reminds readers that LLMs are essentially prediction engines — even a model that has been reliable for months offers no guarantee, especially for anything touching pesticides and food safety. Always double-check with a human expert.
