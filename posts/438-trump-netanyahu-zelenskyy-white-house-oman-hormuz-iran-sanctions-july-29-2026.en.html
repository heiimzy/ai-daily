⚡ July 29 International Affairs: Trump Hosts Back-to-Back Summits — Netanyahu's First White House Visit Since Iran War, Zelenskyy Seeks Patriot Missiles, Oman Submits Hormuz Management Plan

1. Trump Meets Netanyahu and Zelenskyy in Rare Dual White House Summits

President Donald Trump held back-to-back Oval Office meetings on July 28 with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy — a rare diplomatic feat that saw two wartime leaders converge on the White House on the same day.

Netanyahu arrived at the White House at 11:00 AM ET for a closed-door meeting lasting approximately 90 minutes. Vice President JD Vance, Secretary of State Marco Rubio, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth were in attendance. White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt described the talks as "positive and productive" but offered no further details. This was the first face-to-face meeting between the two leaders since the US and Israel jointly launched airstrikes against Iran in late February.

The meeting comes at a critical juncture for Netanyahu, who faces an October 27 election with polls showing him trailing amid public frustration over the prolonged Iran war. Analysts say the visit aims to dispel reports of a rift with Trump, who last April reportedly cursed at Netanyahu during ceasefire negotiations. "Israel would not exist without the United States and my decision to take out Iran's developing nuclear weapons," Trump told reporters aboard Air Force One ahead of the meeting.

Earlier in the day, Zelenskyy met Trump at 9:47 AM ET for a roughly one-hour discussion. Zelenskyy called it a "good meeting," saying the pair discussed licenses for Patriot interceptor production and diplomatic efforts to end Russia's war. Trump earlier this month approved Ukraine's longstanding request to produce US-designed Patriot missiles under license. The meeting marks a significant improvement in relations since their heated Oval Office exchange in February 2025.

Analysts note the strategic timing of Zelenskyy's visit. Ukraine last weekend struck an Iranian commercial vessel in the Caspian Sea carrying military cargo to Russia — an attack that Iran has vowed to avenge. By linking the Ukraine and Middle East theaters, Kyiv gains additional leverage in its quest for US support.

2. Oman Formally Submits Hormuz Joint Management Plan with Gulf Backing

In the most significant diplomatic breakthrough in weeks, Oman has formally presented Iran with a comprehensive proposal for joint management of the Strait of Hormuz, backed by Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states, Reuters reported July 28. The plan is modeled on the Strait of Malacca system, allowing Iran to collect voluntary service fees from transiting vessels while preventing Tehran from exercising exclusive control.

Key elements of the proposal include: Iran would not have unilateral control over the waterway; any fees would be voluntary — mirroring the system used by Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore in the Malacca Strait where ships are asked for voluntary contributions toward navigation services, environmental protection, and search-and-rescue operations; and a regional alliance would oversee maritime security and related operations, funded voluntarily by regional states, the shipping industry, and oil sector.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi discussed the Strait in separate phone calls with his Omani and Saudi counterparts on July 27. An Iranian Foreign Ministry statement described the calls as emphasizing "the need to strengthen cooperation and advance joint diplomatic efforts to establish stability in the region." However, Iran continues to deny direct negotiations with the United States. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei stated that Tehran is not seeking new talks with Washington but merely receiving messages via intermediaries.

At least four major points of contention remain between Oman and Iran, according to Al Jazeera: the transit route (Iran insists on defining it), fee structure (Iran initially demanded mandatory transit fees, later shifted to "maritime service charges," while Oman prefers no fees), whether management should be bilateral or multilateral, and control over mine clearance operations.

3. Iran Takes Hard Line: Strait 'Remains Closed,' 'Not Afraid of Any Action'

Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei stated at his July 28 press briefing that "the Strait of Hormuz remains closed." He emphasized that the strait's management is a strictly bilateral matter between Iran and Oman and "has nothing to do with the United States."

Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi issued a sharper warning via Fars news agency, saying Tehran would consider "any action" to maintain control over the Strait, including resumption of war. He warned that if Iran opened a southern shipping lane closer to the Omani coast, it would "no longer be able to exercise sovereignty." "Any European ship that wants to come near the Strait of Hormuz is our legitimate target," he added.

Deputy Parliament Speaker Haji Babaii further stressed that any arrangement with Oman over the waterway must not clash with Iran's sovereignty and that Washington should not be involved. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) reported intercepting four vessels attempting to transit through unauthorized routes over the past 24 hours, forcing them back with warning shots.

Meanwhile, the US and Iran observed a fourth consecutive night without hostilities. Trump said July 27 that "very friendly negotiations" were underway but warned of returning to "large-scale military operations" if talks fail. Iran continues to deny direct negotiations, insisting all communication passes through Omani, Qatari, and Pakistani mediators.

4. Trump Renews Threat to Strike Iran's Pickaxe Mountain Nuclear Facility; Senate Advances Sanctions Bill

Trump renewed his threat to strike Iran's underground Pickaxe Mountain nuclear facility during a July 28 Fox News interview, saying: "We have the best cameras in the world pointed at it... I know exactly what's at Pickaxe Mountain — it's not that big of a deal. If we can't make a deal, it has to be destroyed." Located near the Natanz nuclear facility in Iran's Isfahan province, Pickaxe Mountain is considered one of Iran's most heavily fortified underground nuclear sites. Trump made a similar threat on July 21.

On Capitol Hill, the Senate is scheduled for a procedural vote on the evening of July 28 on the "Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026." The bipartisan legislation would impose 100% tariffs on goods from the five largest importers of Russian oil and gas — including China and India — and sanction President Vladimir Putin, senior officials, financial institutions, and major energy projects. Zelenskyy last-minute endorsed Trump's push to add Iran to the same sanctions bill, calling it a "great idea."

5. Romania Expels Russian Diplomat; Houthis Threaten Bab al-Mandeb — Regional Spillover Intensifies

Romania expelled a Russian diplomat on July 28 in response to repeated incursions by Russian Shahed drones into Romanian airspace. The NATO member state has shot down three drones over three consecutive days — one of which was confirmed as Russian-made. Romania summoned the Russian ambassador and declared a diplomat persona non grata, giving him five days to leave. Russia's Foreign Ministry denounced the action as "groundless" and vowed a "certain response."

In Yemen, Houthi forces are reportedly preparing to emulate Iran's strategy by attempting to seize control of the Bab al-Mandeb Strait — the other critical global energy chokepoint. Yemen's Foreign Minister-designate Afra Zuba warned that the Houthis are copying Iran's Hormuz playbook, threatening to block both Middle Eastern shipping lanes simultaneously. Data from Kepler shows that Bab al-Mandeb shipping volumes have dropped nearly 50% in the six days since the Houthis announced their maritime blockade against Saudi Arabia.

Trita Parsi, executive vice president of the Quincy Institute, noted that the fundamental dilemma of the US-Iran conflict is that neither side can achieve its objectives through force — leaving negotiation as the only viable path. But the ambiguous wording of the June MoU, particularly paragraph 5 on "free passage" through the strait, remains a fatal fault line between war and peace.
