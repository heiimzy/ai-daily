OpenAI Officially Confirms Astra: Its Next Major Model Family Solves Ten Long-Open Math Problems, All Backed by Lean Formal Proofs

On August 1, OpenAI published "Ten advances in mathematics and theoretical computer science," officially confirming for the first time that its next major model family is called Astra. The company says the results were produced by an internal version of Astra - each problem had seen no substantive progress on its main result for at least a decade, and in most cases much longer. Crucially, all ten results ship with machine-checkable Lean 4 formal proof certificates, open-sourced on GitHub (openai/ten-proofs, Apache 2.0), closing the loop on the biggest failure mode of earlier AI-math claims: plausible conclusions with no verification.

The ten results span seven fields and carry real weight: new upper bounds on high-dimensional sphere packing approaching the Cohn-Elkies threshold; exponentially improved bounds for binary and spherical codes; a construction proving that non-sofic groups exist - settling a central question posed by Mikhail Gromov in 1999 and open for 27 years; a counterexample to Connes' rigidity conjecture; new lower bounds for computing the permanent with arithmetic circuits, including an n⁴/log n formula lower bound; an exponential parallel repetition theorem for two-player quantum games; polynomial-factor hardness of approximation for the closest vector problem (directly relevant to post-quantum cryptography); a resolution of Ehrhart's volume conjecture in every dimension; a superexponential lower bound for multicolor triangle Ramsey numbers (resolving Erdos problem 183); and results on the compactness and degeneracy conjectures in extremal graph theory (Erdos problems 146 and 180).

OpenAI stresses that finding these solutions would cost roughly $2,000 in total tokens at Sol API rates. Human researchers turned Astra's arguments into manuscripts, then the model formalized each argument into a Lean certificate, and the company released the model's narrated reasoning for every problem. OpenAI also addressed authorship unusually directly: claiming human authorship for a proof generated entirely by an AI system would misrepresent both the system's contribution and genuine human intellectual work, it said - so the mathematical arguments are credited to Astra, while humans prepared the manuscripts and formalized the proofs and take responsibility for their correctness. It pointed to the Leiden declaration on AI and Mathematics, signed by mathematicians concerned about how AI-generated results get credited.

Fields medalist Timothy Gowers called the work a milestone for AI-assisted mathematics; Thomas Bloom, who runs ErdosProblems.com, judged the non-sofic construction "bigger than the unit distance counterexample" in terms of mathematical construction; Elliot Glazer, who led the FrontierMath benchmark, called it one of the most important AI-assisted math results to date. OpenAI researcher Noam Brown also acknowledged Astra failed on other major problems. Parameter count, release form (GPT-6 vs GPT-5.7) and pricing remain undecided.

EU AI Act Article 50 Takes Effect Today: Chatbots Must Disclose They Are AI, Deepfakes Must Be Labeled

The transparency obligations under Article 50 of the EU AI Act formally apply from August 2, 2026 (today). The European Commission adopted its final 51-page implementation guidelines on July 20, laying out four duties: first, AI systems that interact directly with people - chatbots, AI assistants, customer-service bots - must inform users at the start of the interaction that they are talking to an AI; a footnote in the terms of service is not enough. Second, generative AI output (text, image, audio, video) must carry machine-readable marks (the industry standard is C2PA signed metadata plus invisible watermarking such as SynthID) so it can be automatically detected as AI-generated - systems already on the market before August 2 get a grace period until December 2. Third, deployers using emotion recognition or biometric categorization systems must inform the people being measured. Fourth, deepfakes and AI-generated text on matters of public interest published without human review must be labeled in a clear, perceivable way as "artificially generated or manipulated."

A notable detail: the deepfake definition does not require intent to deceive. If AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video resembles real people, objects, places or events and would falsely appear authentic, it must be labeled - even with no deceptive intent and even if it depicts a fictional but realistic-looking person. Obviously unrealistic content (flying humans, dragons) is out of scope. Artistic, satirical and fictional works may use a lighter labeling method that does not spoil enjoyment.

Fines for Article 50 breaches fall in the second tier: up to EUR 15 million or 3% of the previous financial year's global annual turnover, whichever is higher - calculated on worldwide, not EU-only, turnover. An important clarification: the "Digital Omnibus" (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, approved by the Council on June 29 and in force since July 27) deferred the high-risk Annex III obligations (hiring, credit, education, biometrics) to December 2, 2027 - but Article 50 transparency was not postponed. Meanwhile, California's AI Transparency Act (SB 942) also takes effect the same day, requiring generative AI providers with over a million monthly California users to embed provenance signals and offer a free detection tool - the "AI transparency era" begins on both sides of the Atlantic on the same day.

Morgan Stanley: Global Cloud Capex Could Approach $1.3 Trillion in 2027, Cloud AI Semiconductor Market Reaching $753B by 2030

Morgan Stanley's latest Greater China semiconductor report (released July 31, widely covered August 1) makes strikingly aggressive AI buildout forecasts: the world's top 14 listed cloud service providers could see combined capex approach $1.3 trillion in 2027 (excluding sovereign AI projects), the cloud AI semiconductor market is projected at $485 billion in 2026 and about $753 billion by 2030, and the global semiconductor market could reach $1.5 trillion by 2030 - with AI semiconductors accounting for nearly half.

The report's core thesis: AI semiconductors remain in a high-growth cycle, and the rally is broadening beyond pure GPU demand to advanced process technology, advanced packaging, memory, testing equipment, ASICs and China's AI chip supply chain. Demand-side evidence includes the big four cloud vendors (AWS, Google, Microsoft, Meta) growing Q1 2026 capex 95% year over year, with full-year cloud capex projected at $685 billion and directly fueling CoWoS/SoIC advanced-packaging queues; Nvidia's export restrictions to China are meanwhile raising the certainty of domestic AI chip substitution, with China's cloud capex expected at $105 billion in 2026, roughly 14% of the global total.

The report also flags risks: hyperscaler capital intensity (capex/sales) is forecast to peak at 44% in 2027, above the 32% dot-com-era peak, and depreciation plus off-balance-sheet lease commitments could pressure margins. But its core stance remains "this is not a short cycle": with roughly 65% of cloud assets short-lived, providers must keep buying every year, giving demand rigidity. For investors, Morgan Stanley recommends focusing on advanced packaging, testing equipment and the China chip chain rather than traditional cyclical logic.
