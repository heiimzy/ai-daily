DeepSeek's Liang Wenfeng: No Overtime, No KPIs, Half the Workday Is Yours — a $60B Lesson in 'Relaxed Management'

A nearly four-hour internal discussion obtained and transcribed by Tencent Technology (widely covered by Fortune and TNW from August 1) reveals that DeepSeek founder Liang Wenfeng told employees the roughly $60 billion AI lab generally does not work overtime and does not use KPIs, with no formal hierarchy, no written rulebook, and no monthly targets. "No one manages them. No KPIs," Liang says in the transcript. "We hope 'formal work' doesn't take more than half of an employee's time. The other half is unallocated — they can do whatever they want." He gives two reasons: research needs a relaxed environment — push too hard and you can't do research — and the company is intensely focused, doing very few things.

The remarks have stirred Silicon Valley precisely because they contrast with the 996 grind Western tech founders are now rushing to adopt. DeepSeek, the ChatGPT rival, has accumulated roughly 173 million downloads since its public launch in January 2025 and grown from an unknown startup to a $60 billion powerhouse in three years; Bloomberg estimates Liang's personal fortune at about $36 billion. Management runs on consensus rather than command: employees are told what to do for only half their day and left to explore the rest. Notably, DeepSeek paused its second fundraising round in late July after Liang's separate comments about China's dependence on Nvidia chips went viral on Weibo without his permission — and this transcript also leaked without authorization.

Analysts note the open question is whether the no-KPI model survives DeepSeek's transition from research lab to public company. Liang himself concedes the firm is run by "a bunch of very ordinary people," but insists: "We generally don't work overtime. First, research needs a relaxed environment; second, we're very focused, so we do very few things." TNW's verdict: a three-year-old startup valued at $60 billion, built by a team whose founder insists nobody works late, is itself the strongest rebuttal to the culture that equates exhaustion with heroism.

Samsung Galaxy Z Fold 8 Sells Out Across All Colors in Korea: Deliveries Slip from August 7 to August 28, MKBHD Calls It a 'Surprise Hit'

Samsung's next-generation foldable family — the Z Fold 8, Z Fold 8 Ultra, and Z Flip 8 — has hit unexpectedly strong demand in its home market. According to Business Post, every color and storage tier of the Galaxy Z Fold 8 is sold out in South Korea, and Samsung Korea has confirmed that new orders will now ship on August 28 instead of the originally promised August 7 — a three-week delay. The 1TB Z Fold 8 Ultra also slips to August 28, the Z Flip 8 to August 11 or 18 depending on color, and offline retail queues stretch into mid-September. A Samsung official said the company cannot confirm the exact cause of the inventory shortage but that shipping dates were delayed "because sales have exceeded expectations."

The Z Fold 8's headline feature is its new "passport-shaped" wide-body design with a 4:3 inner display. Pre-orders are reportedly running at roughly three times the level of last year's Z Fold 7 and approaching 80% of the Galaxy S26's volume — a record for the foldable line. Tech YouTuber Marques Brownlee (MKBHD), in a review published August 1, was notably positive: crease reduction is dramatically improved (he rated Oppo's Find N6 a 10/10, the new Samsung 8–8.5, and last year's model only 5), and he admitted "the more I use it... the more I'm liking it," saying he could see himself using it daily. He also flagged the trade-offs: the phone drops the telephoto lens entirely (50MP main plus 50MP ultrawide only), has no Flex Mode, and some apps render poorly on the new aspect ratio. The Z Fold 8 starts at $1,899 and goes on sale August 7.

Industry observers note Samsung reportedly built more Fold 8 units than the Ultra and Flip combined, betting the new form factor can reach a broader mainstream audience. The "sold out across the board plus three-week delay" in Korea is being read as fresh evidence that the foldable category is heating back up amid the AI-phone wave.

Google Kills Standalone AI Studio App: 800K Pre-Registrations Folded into Gemini's 950M Monthly Users

Google on July 31 officially canceled its standalone AI Studio mobile app, despite roughly 800,000 pre-registrations spanning 168 countries and regions. According to The Verge, AI Studio's core builder capabilities will be integrated into the Gemini app, which has about 950 million monthly active users and serves as Google's super-app gateway. The decision reflects Google's product philosophy of "don't make users download a new app — embed the capability into channels they already use," trading a standalone brand for Gemini's massive user base as a natural distribution channel, delivering higher distribution efficiency and lower marginal cost.

The move stands in sharp contrast to OpenAI's "app matrix" approach, in which ChatGPT, Sora, and Operator ship as separate brands. Google's bet is that developer-oriented tools — code generation, prompt debugging, model comparison — have value for everyday users too, so why force developers to install another app when they already live in Gemini? The risk is equally clear: AI Studio's ecosystem counts roughly 13 million developers, and the core 800K pre-registrants could be relegated to a "second-level menu" inside Gemini, weakening Google's position in developer mindshare; a standalone brand's recognition among power users is something Gemini cannot fully replace.

Industry analysts see the move as part of Google's broader super-app strategy — alongside Gemini Drops monthly updates and system-wide voice control on macOS, the company keeps pushing AI capabilities to the front of users' existing workflows. Killing the AI Studio app is, at bottom, redefining a "builder's tool" as an "AI capability layer for everyone."
