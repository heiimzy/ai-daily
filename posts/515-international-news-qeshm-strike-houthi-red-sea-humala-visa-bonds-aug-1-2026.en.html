US 2,000-lb Bomb Hits Family Home in Qeshm, Iran, Killing Three

A visual analysis by The New York Times and weapons experts suggests the US dropped a 2,000-pound (about 907 kg) Mark-84 bomb on a family home on Iran's Qeshm island on the night of July 30, killing a husband, his wife and their two-year-old son, while two other children were pulled alive from the rubble (NYT visual analysis, via The Straits Times, published 1:59 PM SGT / 13:59 Beijing time on August 1, updated 2:01 PM).

The strike hit the Chah-Tangu neighbourhood of Qeshm City — a densely packed residential area — leaving a crater at least 9 metres wide, displacing roughly 20 households, and hurling debris and damaged vehicles more than 60 metres away. Two warehouses outside the city were also destroyed in the same wave of attacks. Iranian officials said the air raids were part of US retaliation for an earlier Iranian strike on a base used by US forces in Jordan; but The Times found no indication of a military site near the family's home and no reports of military casualties, raising questions about why such a destructive munition was used over a populated area.

"We are aware of the reports and looking into them. The US military never targets civilians." — Captain Tim Hawkins, US Central Command (CENTCOM) spokesman

Trevor Ball, a former US Army explosive ordnance disposal technician and analyst at Armament Research Services, said the crater and structural damage are consistent with a 2,000-pound Mark-84 bomb that can be fitted with JDAM guidance kits. Experts warn that using heavy ordnance in civilian neighbourhoods may breach international humanitarian law. Qeshm sits in the Strait of Hormuz, which has been largely shut since the US-Israel war on Iran began earlier this year, keeping Middle East maritime tensions high.

Houthis Deny Red Sea Transit-Fee Plan; Saudi Arabia Leads 14-Nation Maritime Coalition

Yemen's Houthi rebels, through their Humanitarian Operations Coordination Center (HOCC), denied on Saturday (August 1) any plan to charge ships transiting the Red Sea, saying their "safe transit service" is voluntary and free, that anyone demanding payment "does not represent Yemen or the HOCC," and urging shipping companies not to pay or share information with unauthorized parties (Al Jazeera/Reuters, published 11:33 Beijing time August 1).

The denial follows a Reuters report on Wednesday, citing regional sources, that the Houthis were considering imposing fees on vessels transiting the Bab al-Mandeb Strait — an idea raised with Iranian officials during a Houthi visit to Tehran earlier in July, with Iranian advisers reportedly helping set up an authority to regulate the fees. Yemen's foreign minister-designate, Afrah al-Zouba, said on Tuesday the Houthis were seeking to "copy the Iranian model": Iran has tried to charge ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz, which has been largely closed since the war on Iran began.

  • Bab al-Mandeb links the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden — a vital chokepoint for oil and cargo moving between Asia, Europe and the Middle East;
  • Saudi Arabia this week announced a 14-nation maritime coalition to protect freedom of navigation in the strait, the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden;
  • A UN expert report found the Houthis may have collected significant informal fees from shipping agents in 2024 (never independently verified);
  • Traffic through the strait has still not recovered to pre-2023 levels.

A "toll-ified" Bab al-Mandeb would hit Saudi Arabia especially hard, as the kingdom has increasingly relied on the strait as an alternative export route while Hormuz remains disrupted. Red Sea shipping security remains a core variable in Middle East geopolitics.

Peru's Ex-President Humala Released After Conviction Overturned

Peru's Constitutional Court overturned former president Ollanta Humala's 15-year prison sentence for money laundering, and on August 1 Humala walked free from the Barbadillo special detention facility in eastern Lima that houses several of Peru's jailed former leaders (Al Jazeera/AFP, published August 1). The 64-year-old, who led Peru from 2011 to 2016, and his wife Nadine Heredia were convicted last year of money laundering for receiving illegal campaign contributions from Brazilian construction giant Odebrecht (now Novonor) and the Venezuelan government in two presidential campaigns; Heredia subsequently received asylum in Brazil.

"It has been decided to declare the entire criminal proceedings against Ollanta Moises Humala Tasso null and void," read the court ruling dated July 15 and published on Thursday (July 30). The court found his habeas corpus petition "well-founded" because he was convicted for conduct that was not legally defined as money laundering when the contributions were received. His lawyer, Wilfredo Pedraza, said he received the judgement "with considerable satisfaction."

Humala was the first Peruvian ex-leader to face trial in the Odebrecht corruption scandal, which has also tainted three other former presidents. In 2016, Odebrecht agreed to pay $3.5 billion in penalties after admitting to $788 million in bribes to foreign leaders and officials worldwide to win infrastructure projects — including at least $29 million paid to Peruvian officials between 2005 and 2014.

US Makes Visa-Bond Program Permanent: Travelers from 50 Countries Face Up to $20,000 Bonds

The US State Department will make permanent a visa bond program that could require applicants from 50 countries — 30 of them in Africa — to post bonds of up to US$20,000 (S$25,700) when seeking B1/B2 business and tourism visas, according to a Federal Register notice posted online on July 31 (The Straits Times/Reuters, published 11:16 AM SGT / 11:16 Beijing time August 1, updated 1:03 PM). The final rule takes effect on August 3.

The 2025 pilot program let consular officers require bonds of $5,000, $10,000 or up to $15,000; the final rule drops the $5,000 option and raises the cap to $20,000. US officials say the policy is intended to reduce visa overstays, while immigration advocates argue it will deter legitimate travel and criticize the Trump administration for tightening legal immigration through new fees and social-media vetting even as it cracks down on illegal immigration.
