Monday, July 27, saw multiple seismic shifts across the Middle Eastern geopolitical landscape within hours. I. Historic Signal: Syria's President Proposes Security Deal with Israel

Syrian President Ahmad al-Sharaa told Al Jazeera in an exclusive, wide-ranging interview that Damascus is seeking to reach a security agreement with Israel, which could pave the way for comprehensive peace between the two countries. This marks the first time a Syrian leader has made such an explicit positive statement toward normalized relations since the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024.

Al-Sharaa stated that any deal must not compromise Syria's right to the Golan Heights, the strategic plateau Israel captured from Syria in the 1967 Six-Day War and annexed in 1981. He said Syria was trying to involve a large group of countries to pressure Israel toward a more balanced policy, adding: "We are avoiding getting into confrontations."

"A security deal could pave the way for comprehensive peace between the two countries without compromising Syria's right to the Golan Heights," al-Sharaa said. He also categorically ruled out military intervention in Lebanon, saying Damascus is working to enable the Lebanese state to fulfill its commitments to disarm Hezbollah while preventing spillover into Syria.

The interview coincided with UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres's visit to Damascus — the first by a UN chief to Syria since 2009. Guterres called for respect for Syria's sovereignty and territorial integrity, stating that "violations by Israel of the 1974 Disengagement of Forces Agreement are unacceptable and must stop."

Israel's Response and Ongoing Tensions

Even as al-Sharaa spoke, Israeli forces shelled targets inside Syria, destroying an electrical transformer in the village of Maariyah and depriving homes of power, according to Syrian state news agency SANA. Since Assad's fall, Israeli troops have moved into the demilitarized zone inside Syria — including the Syrian side of Mount Hermon overlooking Damascus — and have conducted strikes and detentions inside the country. Israel has also intervened under the banner of protecting the Druze minority in southwestern Syria.

Analysts caution that while al-Sharaa's proposal is diplomatically significant, the road to any substantive agreement remains extremely long given the Golan sovereignty dispute, Israel's ongoing military presence inside Syria, and the complex Hezbollah factor. The statement is more likely the starting point of a long-term diplomatic dance rather than a breakthrough.

II. Strait of Hormuz: Tanker Hits Mine, Oman Pushes New Management Framework

Against the backdrop of two consecutive nights without US-Iran airstrikes, the Strait of Hormuz saw fresh trouble early Monday. An oil tanker attempting to cross the strategic waterway struck a naval mine and exploded after straying from Iran-approved shipping lanes, according to Iranian state broadcaster IRIB. The vessel had turned off its navigation and positioning systems while attempting to traverse a route in the southern part of the strait that Iran deemed "illegal and unsafe." Six ships were intercepted and turned back by Iranian authorities.

On the diplomatic front, an Omani delegation held talks in Tehran on Monday, exchanging proposals on a mechanism for the future management of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. According to Al Jazeera, Oman proposed a regional management structure, but Iran continues to insist on retaining control of the waterway and charging a "service fee." Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmail Baghaei confirmed that talks had made progress and would continue.

Shipping data from analytics platform Kpler showed traffic at three-week lows: only three vessels transited on Saturday with their transponders off, and seven on Sunday. A US Navy maritime body confirmed no new attacks in the past 72 hours but warned that commercial shipping remained at significant risk.

US Ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz stated Sunday that Trump paused strikes to "give talks some space." However, the US military confirmed Monday that its recently reimposed naval blockade against Iran continues — with a dozen commercial ships redirected, two disabled, and two boarded — indicating that while airstrikes have paused, the maritime confrontation remains very much active.

Brent crude briefly dipped below $90/barrel in Asian trading Monday, sliding as much as 7%, before recovering to around $92. Markets remain cautiously optimistic about the diplomatic window, but the Houthi blockade threat in the Red Sea continues to inject significant risk premiums into oil prices.

III. Pentagon Controversy: 4 Fallen Troops Removed from Official Iran War Tally

The Associated Press reported Monday that four US service members killed during the recent escalation of fighting between the US and Iran have been removed from Washington's official war casualty count and reclassified under a newly designated category called "Overseas Operations." Along with the four deceased, 207 wounded personnel were also transferred out of the main tally.

The move has sparked controversy over how the Trump administration is defining and narrating the Iran war. The Defense Casualty Analysis System, previously used by military officials as the primary record for casualties in the conflict, now omits these four names from its main count.

The New York Times reported that Joint Chiefs Chairman General Dan Caine privately warned Trump in a high-level Friday meeting that continuing major combat operations against Iran would dangerously strain interceptor supplies allocated to US Central Command. The Times also reported that plans to escalate the US campaign had been shelved in part because of shrinking munitions supplies.

White House Communications Director Steven Cheung emphasized that Trump has always preferred diplomacy. But Senator Chris Van Hollen lambasted the administration Sunday, saying: "We should never have gotten into this senseless war, and we should end it now." Senator Bernie Sanders accused the administration of depleting missile defenses, draining oil reserves, and sacrificing American lives "all for Israel."

According to Wall Street Journal analysis citing CSIS estimates, at least 1,500 Patriot interceptors have been expended since the conflict began, with fewer than 1,000 estimated remaining. US Central Command chief Admiral Brad Cooper reportedly told Trump that the two-week bombing campaign had "largely exhausted the pre-selected target list," with diminishing returns from continued limited strikes.

IV. West Bank: Largest Crackdown in Years, Over 120 Detained

Beyond the Gaza front, Israel's military operations in the occupied West Bank have escalated dramatically. According to Al Jazeera, Israeli forces and settlers have launched the largest coordinated assault on West Bank towns and villages in years over the past 48 hours — raiding areas around Nablus, Ramallah, Hebron, and Jerusalem, and detaining more than 120 Palestinians.

The immediate trigger was a Friday firefight near Nablus that left four Palestinians and two Israeli soldiers dead. Israeli forces subsequently raided the village of Tal on Monday and arrested the father of two of the Palestinians killed in the incident. Settler violence has also intensified — marauding through villages, spray-painting buildings, and attacking Palestinian civilians.

Palestinian President Mahmoud Abbas urged world leaders to take immediate action to halt the escalating attacks. The UAE on Monday "strongly condemned" the settler attacks, calling the arson of a mosque in Tulkarem "provocative and inciting."

The West Bank crackdown comes as Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu departed for Washington on Monday for a Tuesday meeting with President Trump. Analysts note that Netanyahu's dual strategy — showing strength in the West Bank to consolidate right-wing domestic support while seeking strategic concessions from Washington — is a familiar playbook.
