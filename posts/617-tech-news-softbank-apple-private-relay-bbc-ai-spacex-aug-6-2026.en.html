⚡ Aug 6 Tech Briefing: SoftBank Beats, Apple Private Relay Leaks IP, BBC Fact-Checks AI Disaster Videos, SpaceX Employees Cash In

Over the past hour or two, four stories dominated the tech world: SoftBank reported fiscal Q1 profit of 347.3B yen ($2.2B), far above expectations, thanks to surging values of its Intel and ByteDance stakes; security researchers revealed that Apple's iCloud Private Relay can leak your real IP address through a WebKit flaw when you sign in with passkeys — affecting every iOS browser; BBC Verify published an investigation breaking down AI-generated flood videos circulating on Chinese social media; and SpaceX's first 20% of shares unlocked on August 6, prompting early employees to start cashing out. Here is this round's tech briefing.

1. SoftBank's Q1 profit crushes estimates on 1.3T yen Intel gain

According to CNBC's August 6 report, SoftBank reported net profit of 347.3 billion yen ($2.2B) for its fiscal first quarter (ended June), well above the 120.23 billion yen analysts had expected (per LSEG estimates) — though still down nearly 18% year on year. The main driver was a huge gain on SoftBank's stake in Intel.

SoftBank announced a roughly $2 billion investment in Intel last year, and this quarter that holding produced a 1.3 trillion yen gain, helping its investment division book segment profit of 1.05 trillion yen. Meanwhile, the Vision Funds — which house investments from OpenAI to TikTok-owner ByteDance — saw a $1.7 billion gain in value in the quarter, driven primarily by a $2.2 billion increase in the value of SoftBank's ByteDance stake, which offset declines in names like PayPal. Analysts see the results as confirmation that SoftBank is doubling down on a "chips + AI applications" double play: betting on the semiconductor cycle through Intel while riding the AI-app valuation repricing through ByteDance and OpenAI.

2. Apple's Private Relay can leak your IP: passkeys are the hole

According to an Engadget report on August 6, security researchers Talal Haj Bakry and Tommy Mysk found that Apple's iCloud Private Relay doesn't always work as designed — due to a flaw in the WebKit engine, your real IP address can be exposed to websites and services when you sign in with a passkey. Private Relay is supposed to ensure that no website, or even Apple itself, can see your IP address when you browse in Safari.

The problem, the researchers explain, is the passkey login flow: when you authenticate with a passkey, your device makes a request outside the browser itself, and that request bypasses Private Relay's proxy tunnel, leaking your real IP. Worse, the issue is not confined to Safari — because every browser on iOS is built on WebKit, users of privacy-focused browsers like OnionBrowser can leak their IP too when using passkeys. The researchers said on X that they have already contacted OnionBrowser and the Tor Project to share their findings and fixes; Apple told 404Media it is investigating the report, without offering a fix timeline yet. For users who rely on Apple's privacy pitch, this is a security reminder worth watching.

3. BBC Verify breaks down AI fake flood videos spreading in China

According to a 1:41 investigation video published by the BBC on August 6, BBC Verify analyzed viral "flood scene" videos circulating on Chinese social media and confirmed that some of the footage was generated by AI. The video's title asks directly: "AI or real?" — as weather events become more extreme, these fabricated videos are spreading rapidly online and causing real-world problems in China.

The BBC notes that during disaster and emergency situations, deepfakes carry outsized damage: they can stoke panic, disrupt rescue information, and even be weaponized for disinformation campaigns. The report urges platforms to strengthen AI-content labeling and provenance verification, and reminds ordinary users to check the source, capture time, and verifiable details in any "disaster scene" video before resharing. This is not an isolated case — AI-generated disaster imagery has appeared after multiple natural disasters around the world this year, and AI content governance is moving from "can we make it" to "how do we tell."

4. SpaceX's first 20% unlock: early employees start cashing out

According to a BBC report on August 6, the first 20% of SpaceX's shares unlocked on August 6, and several early employees have begun selling in stages. Andre Lavoie, 63, joined SpaceX as an engineer in 2009; the roughly 200,000 shares he was granted back then are now worth about $23M (£17M). He told the BBC: "Every chance I get going forward, I'll sell a little bit more… The shares have been going up so radically it keeps messing up my life plans — you really can't know the future, so it's better to sell early and in intervals."

Lavoie is far from alone: Elon Musk said on Fox News that SpaceX's June listing had likely made "several thousand" employees millionaires — including staff on the production line; reports estimate the IPO created about 4,400 new millionaires. Unlike most newly listed firms, SpaceX's shares are released in stages: the first 20% on August 6, with more batches through the rest of the year. The company listed on the Nasdaq in June in the biggest IPO in history, valuing it at more than $2 trillion and briefly making Musk the world's first trillionaire before the stock cooled. In its first results as a public company this week, quarterly revenue nearly doubled year on year to $7.8B, while spending ballooned to $18.3B — more than six times a year ago; the firm posted a net loss of $143M in the quarter and $2B in the first half. Musk pushed back on the earnings call: "I think people are really underestimating Starlink," predicting the satellite internet business — the one part of the company currently profitable — could one day carry a majority of the world's internet. Still, shares tumbled after the report, with investors spooked by the huge AI-related spending.
