⚡ July 26 Financial Bombshell: Trump Halts Iran Strikes Sending Oil Crashing — Brent Dives from $102 to $89, Inflation Fears Ease, Fed Hike Odds Plummet

For two weeks, the U.S. military launched nightly airstrikes on Iran, the Strait of Hormuz ground to a near-halt, and Brent crude surged past $100 a barrel, plunging global markets into a vicious cycle of "oil shock → inflation spike → forced Fed tightening." Then came a sudden reversal that rewrote the narrative: President Trump ordered a halt to all military strikes on Iran on July 24, and an Omani mediation delegation arrived in Tehran to negotiate the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. In Asian trading on July 26, oil prices plunged — Brent crude crashed from an intraday high of $102 to $89.56, a drop of over 12%, while WTI fell to $86.62. Global markets caught a desperately needed breather.

I. 13 Nights of Strikes Halt: Why Did Trump Slam the Brakes?

On the afternoon of July 24 (Friday), Trump convened a decisive meeting with top advisers and senior Cabinet members. On the table was a multi-phase plan for a major bombing campaign targeting Iran's coastal radars, anti-ship missile launchers, and IRGC fast-boat fleets. The outcome surprised everyone: the plan was rejected, and all U.S. airstrikes on Iran were suspended.

Three Forces Pushed Trump to Pivot

  • Patriot Interceptor Stockpiles Running Dangerously Low: According to Business Times citing two officials briefed on the discussion, General Dan Caine, Chair of the U.S. Joint Chiefs of Staff, privately warned that resuming major combat operations would dangerously deplete interceptors available to CENTCOM. The Pentagon had already used over 1,200 Patriot interceptors (at $4 million each) by late April, leaving inventories worrisomely low. On July 17, three U.S. soldiers were killed in Jordan when a ballistic missile slipped through air defenses — a stark demonstration of the system's vulnerability.
  • Unbearable Political Costs: With the 2026 midterm elections approaching, surging oil prices pushed U.S. gas prices back above $4 per gallon, battering consumer confidence. Internal White House assessments concluded that sustained oil above $100 could cost Republicans control of both the House and Senate.
  • Questionable Military Effectiveness: A senior U.S. official speaking on condition of anonymity said 13 consecutive nights of strikes had "the opposite effect of what was intended" — rather than deterring Iran, the attacks allowed Iranian leaders to focus attention on an external threat and maintain domestic cohesion. Few in Trump's inner circle believed escalation was wise.

White House Communications Director Steven Cheung said the president "has always been consistent in saying he prefers a diplomatic solution," while retaining all options if Iran continues "terrorist activities in the Strait of Hormuz or against allies."

"Look, we're locked and loaded. We're ready to go, but we're talking to them, so maybe there will be, maybe there won't be a tipping point." — Trump to reporters on July 24

II. Oil's Cliff Dive: Brent Crashes from $102 to $89

Peace hopes proved the best oil price depressant. In Asian trading on July 26, crude markets experienced a violent selloff:

  • Brent Crude: Plunged from an intraday high of $102 to close near $89.56, a single-day drop exceeding 12%;
  • WTI Crude: Fell 3.86% to $86.62/barrel, completely reversing the surge above $100;
  • Weekly Context: Despite Friday's crash, Brent still posted a weekly gain of approximately 5.39%, and its July rally still stands at roughly 35%.

The oil price surge began on July 10 when U.S.-Iran hostilities escalated sharply. The Houthis announced a blockade on Saudi Red Sea shipping on July 20, claimed attacks on two Saudi oil tankers on July 22, and on July 25 launched missile strikes on Saudi Aramco's Jizan and Yanbu refinery complexes. Combined with a 60%+ drop in Strait of Hormuz transit volumes, Brent surged from $72 to $102 in just two weeks.

Now, with Trump halting strikes and Oman brokering talks, the geopolitical risk premium is rapidly being priced out. Crucially, Qatar announced on July 26 the full resumption of all maritime navigation, ending its half-month shipping ban and further easing Gulf shipping tensions.

III. Fed Rate Hike Odds Collapse: From 82% to Sharp Decline

Oil prices have become the master variable driving global inflation expectations and central bank policy — and this transmission chain has now experienced a dramatic reversal:

Before: Oil Surge Pushed Hike Odds Higher

Just days ago, CME FedWatch showed September rate hike odds at 82%. Brent's surge above $100 pushed U.S. gasoline, chemicals, and consumer goods costs higher, rekindling inflation alarms. The resulting tightening expectations crushed tech stocks, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index falling 17% in July alone.

Now: Oil Crash → Inflation Fears Cool → Hike Odds Retreat

With oil prices collapsing, the market logic has completely inverted. The inflation threat has weakened, the urgency for Fed tightening has dropped sharply, and both the dollar and Treasury yields have pulled back. Safe-haven money that had been piling into gold and oil is starting to rotate back into equities, particularly tech and growth stocks.

Traders caution this rebound is sentiment-driven and likely temporary. The real test comes this week with megacap tech earnings — Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and Apple all report on July 29-30. AI capital expenditure returns will be under intense scrutiny. If big tech delivers disappointing results, the brief recovery will evaporate quickly.

IV. Gold's Awkward Position: Double Squeezed by Geopolitics and Rate Expectations

Gold finds itself in an unprecedented bind. Traditionally, geopolitical conflict should boost gold prices, but this cycle has completely upended that logic:

  • During Conflict: Oil surge → inflation expectations rise → rate hike odds increase → dollar strengthens, yields spike → non-yielding gold declines. On July 23, gold slumped over 2% to $4,049, down approximately 23% from its all-time high earlier this year.
  • After De-escalation: Geopolitical safe-haven demand fades → gold loses support → while lower rate expectations would normally help gold, capital is flowing directly into risk assets (equities) rather than gold. Spot gold currently trades at $4,052/oz, hovering above the key $4,000 support level.

ING analysts note gold is likely to trade in a $4,000-4,200 range near-term, with $4,000 serving as the critical psychological support. The People's Bank of China added 480,000 ounces of gold to its reserves in June (reaching 75.44 million ounces), marking 20 consecutive months of purchases, providing long-term structural support.

V. Deep Restructuring: Seven Pipelines Racing to Bypass Hormuz

This conflict has delivered a painful lesson to Gulf states about the vulnerability of single-chokepoint energy routes. The Strait of Hormuz handles over 14 million barrels of oil per day (~30% of global seaborne crude). When it closes, national economies are immediately strangled. A massive "de-Hormuz" infrastructure race is underway:

  • Saudi Arabia: Its east-west pipeline can carry up to 7 million bpd from eastern fields to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, but Red Sea exports remain threatened by Houthi-controlled Bab al-Mandeb;
  • UAE: Investing $3 billion to expand Fujairah port (outside Hormuz) with parallel pipelines, aiming to double non-Hormuz export capacity to 3.6 million bpd by 2027;
  • Iraq: Deploying oil tanker trucks to Syrian Mediterranean ports while repairing old pipelines and building a new export route to Turkey;
  • Kuwait: Actively negotiating cross-border pipeline capacity expansions.

Kpler analysts note that while Gulf states have historically relied on seaborne oil exports for cost efficiency, the repeated conflicts have proven that single-route dependence is dangerously fragile. A diversified multi-port, multi-modal export system will become standard across the region.

VI. This Week's Risk Calendar: FOMC + Big Tech Earnings + GDP + PCE

While the Middle East has seen a temporary de-escalation, markets are far from safe. The last week of July (July 27-31) is the most densely packed "Super Week" of 2026:

  • July 28-29 FOMC Meeting: Fed rate decision + Chair Warsh press conference. Consensus expects a hold, but the statement language and Warsh's tone will determine the September rate path.
  • July 29 (Wed) After Close: Microsoft and Meta earnings;
  • July 30 (Thu): U.S. Q2 GDP advance estimate, Amazon and Apple earnings, Bank of England rate decision;
  • July 31 (Fri): U.S. June core PCE inflation, Bank of Japan rate decision.

Three narrative threads will determine the direction of global asset prices: whether Brent crude can hold above $90, whether Oman-mediated Hormuz talks produce tangible results, and whether AI megacaps can justify their trillion-dollar capital expenditure commitments with real revenue growth. Markets are currently balanced at a precarious equilibrium — between the temporary relief of geopolitical détente and the daunting reality of the biggest earnings and policy test of 2026.
