⚡ Aug 3 Tech Update: Visa Buys BioCatch for $2.4B, Olix Raises $312M at $3.3B Valuation, Amap Runs a World Model for 24 Hours on One Consumer GPU

August 3 was a busy day across tech and finance: payment giant Visa is spending $2.4 billion to round out its fraud-prevention portfolio, UK AI-chip startup Olix raised $312 million in Europe's largest chip round, and Amap pushed an interactive world model onto a single consumer GPU. The three stories point to fraud defense, AI compute, and the consumer-grade deployment of generative AI — here is the roundup.

1. Visa Agrees to Acquire BioCatch for $2.4 Billion in Cash

On August 3, global payments leader Visa announced it has signed a definitive agreement to acquire BioCatch, a leading provider of behavioral-first, multi-signal fraud intelligence, from funds advised by Permira and other shareholders for $2.4 billion in cash. The transaction is expected to close by the end of Visa's fiscal second quarter of 2027, subject to customary closing conditions including regulatory approvals.

Founded in Tel Aviv, Israel, BioCatch builds AI and machine-learning solutions that analyze thousands of application, behavioral, device and network signals — such as keystrokes, touch gestures and device handling — to detect fraud and distinguish legitimate users from fraudsters in real time. Its products protect 1.8 billion devices and 760 million users worldwide, serving more than 350 banking clients across 21 countries, including over 100 of the largest banks globally.

Visa said the acquisition complements its existing cyber, fraud, risk and security solutions, helping clients defend against the growing threat of account takeovers, scams, money mules and application fraud. Over the past five years, Visa has invested more than $13 billion in technology and infrastructure to safeguard the payments ecosystem and accelerate the decline in fraud rates.

2. Olix Raises $312M at a $3.3B Valuation, Europe's Largest AI Chip Round

On the same day, London-based AI chip startup Olix announced a $312 million Series B round, lifting its valuation from roughly $1 billion in February to $3.3 billion — the largest AI chip funding round in European history. The round was led by New York-based VC Fundomo, with participation from chip giant Arm, quant trading firm Hudson River Trading, and Netflix co-founder Reed Hastings; the UK government's Sovereign AI venture fund also took part.

Founded two years ago by 25-year-old entrepreneur James Dacombe, Olix is building the infrastructure for frontier AI inference — from chips and lasers to the network connecting them. Its key differentiator is a photonic interconnect that moves data between large chip clusters faster and with greater energy efficiency using light, while avoiding the most scarce semiconductor components such as leading-edge silicon, HBM and advanced packaging.

The company plans to tape out its chips later this year and deliver its first products to customers in the second half of 2027, alongside a server-rack offering that packages its chips with software and networking gear. Against a backdrop of recent pullbacks in listed semiconductor stocks and Wall Street's caution over debt-fueled AI infrastructure spending, the round shows private-market enthusiasm for AI silicon remains strong.

3. Amap's ABot-World-0: 24 Hours of Continuous World-Model Inference on One Consumer GPU

At the intersection of research and product, Alibaba's Amap team announced that its open-source interactive world model ABot-World-0 now sustains a single continuous inference session for up to 24 hours — up from the roughly one-minute ceiling of mainstream interactive world models — on just one consumer-grade GPU.

The breakthrough is powered by LongForcing, a training method introduced with ABot-World-0 that keeps image quality, physical consistency and interaction responsiveness stable across day-long generation. In optimized low-bit configurations, ABot-World-0 streams 720p video at up to 16 FPS on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 desktop card, with about 1.2 seconds between an input action and the first response frame, and roughly 19 GiB of peak video memory.

The bigger picture is "compute demotion": long-horizon interactive video generation, previously a data-center workload, now runs on a desktop card — lowering the cost of entry for independent developers, studios and research institutions, and providing a cheaper open-source foundation for embodied AI policy training, game design, interactive video and simulation training.
