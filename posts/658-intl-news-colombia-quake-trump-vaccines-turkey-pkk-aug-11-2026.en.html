⚡ Aug 11 World Update: Colombia Quake Kills 132, Trump Overhauls Childhood Vaccines, Turkey Passes PKK Amnesty Law

Three major international stories broke overnight into Tuesday morning: a magnitude 7.4 earthquake in Colombia's Chocó province — the country's largest in years — has killed at least 132 people and injured more than 480, with at least 40 dead in Pereira, 65 buildings collapsed, and a national disaster declared by a president sworn in just three days ago; US President Donald Trump signed an executive order cutting recommended childhood vaccines from 18 to 11 and recommending the MMR shot be split into separate doses, triggering fierce pushback from the medical community and a Republican senator; and Turkey's parliament passed a landmark bill by 468 votes to 88 giving Kurdish Workers' Party (PKK) members who lay down their arms a path back to civilian life, with some 3,500 detainees expected to be released in the first phase. Details below; all source publish times have been verified.

1. Colombia's 7.4 quake: at least 132 dead, 480+ injured, national disaster declared

According to BBC News (updated 01:59 GMT on Aug 11, marked "20 minutes ago"), Colombia has been hit by one of its largest earthquakes in years, with at least 132 people dead and the toll likely to rise as more people are feared trapped under collapsed buildings. The country's geological service said the magnitude 7.4 quake struck at 07:34 local time (12:34 GMT) at a depth of 103 km (64 miles), with the epicentre near the village of San José del Palmar in Chocó province. It was felt across hundreds of miles of western Colombia, and tremors were felt as far away as neighbouring Venezuela, Ecuador and Panama.

The heaviest toll is in the coffee-growing city of Pereira, about 55 km from the epicentre: local authorities report at least 40 dead in the metropolitan area, 65 collapsed buildings, with people believed trapped in 15 of them, and a curfew in parts of the city until 05:00 Tuesday. In Cali, Colombia's third most populous city, 32 buildings collapsed; Manizales reported at least two deaths, with strict seismic-resistant building codes credited for limiting damage. Colombia's Association of Capital Cities said more than 480 people were injured; the president confirmed more than 1,500 homes damaged, and regional airports in Pereira, Manizales, Quibdó, Armenia, Cartago and Buenaventura reported damage. The US Geological Survey estimates about 1.5 million people felt strong shaking close to magnitude seven.

President Abelardo de la Espriella, sworn in only last Friday, declared a national disaster situation, granting special powers including budget reallocation — a potentially legacy-defining test just three days into his term. World leaders moved quickly to offer support: US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Washington was "closely monitoring" and "ready to support", EU chief Ursula von der Leyen said the bloc's Copernicus satellite was being used in the rescue operation, and El Salvador's President Nayib Bukele offered personnel and equipment. The quake comes less than two months after twin quakes devastated northern Venezuela, killing more than 6,300 people (Sources: BBC; The Guardian live).

2. Trump signs order to cut childhood vaccines from 18 to 11 and split MMR

According to BBC News (published 02:10 GMT Aug 11), Al Jazeera (02:08 GMT) and The Guardian (updated 02:00 GMT), President Trump on Monday signed an executive order calling for fewer childhood vaccines and recommending that the combined measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) shot be split into separate doses. Speaking in the Oval Office, Trump said his administration "is recognising gold standard childhood vaccine recommendations for only 11 core vaccinations" — covering measles, mumps, rubella, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, Haemophilus influenzae type B, pneumococcal disease, HPV and varicella — down from the 18 recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP).

Trump repeated a long-discredited claim linking vaccines to autism: "Decades ago, children received only a small fraction of the vaccines required today. In those times, people were much healthier and of course the high rates of autism now observed did not exist." He even suggested the MMR vaccine could be "quite lethal" if given at once, comparing it to pouring a bottle of soda into a child's body. The US CDC states there is "no published scientific evidence that shows any benefit in separating the combination MMR vaccine into three individual shots" and that vaccination is much safer than contracting measles, mumps or rubella. Experts warn that spacing out jabs leaves children more vulnerable to disease in the interim and increases the chance they miss appointments. Administration officials said children would receive far fewer than the 72 shots under the previous schedule — a figure experts say was inflated by counting combination vaccines as multiple injections and assuming annual flu and Covid-19 doses.

The order drew sharp rebukes. Dr Andrew Racine, president of the American Academy of Pediatrics, called it "disheartening" and "dangerous", noting it comes as US measles cases hit a 35-year high. Republican Senator Bill Cassidy — a physician who chairs the Senate health committee — labelled it "wrong": "The President does not have the expertise to make these changes. Vaccines are overwhelmingly safe. Vaccines are effective. Vaccines DO NOT cause autism." The order also calls for developing alternatives to aluminium in vaccines; the AAP calls the aluminium-autism link "a longstanding rumor". Notably, the federal government does not have the authority to implement the new recommendations — school-entry vaccination requirements are set at the state level, so the order's real-world impact remains to be seen (Sources: BBC; The Guardian).

3. Turkey passes landmark law offering PKK members a path back to civilian life

According to Al Jazeera (video newsfeed, 00:55 GMT Aug 11) and Deutsche Welle (22:22 GMT Aug 10), Turkey's Grand National Assembly passed a historic bill by 468 votes in favour, 88 against and 6 abstentions, offering members of the Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK) who lay down their weapons a chance to return to civilian life — without granting a total amnesty. The law suspends prosecutions and prison sentences for some offences for between five and 10 years, backed by President Recep Tayyip Erdogan's ruling AK Party and the pro-Kurdish DEM Party. Lawmakers say the move is expected to involve the release of around 3,500 PKK-linked detainees in an initial phase — about a third of those held in Turkish prisons.

The vote is Turkey's first legislative step towards ending the decades-long conflict, coming 18 months after jailed PKK founder Abdullah Ocalan urged his followers to disarm. The PKK, designated a "terrorist" organisation by Turkey, the United States and the European Union, launched its rebellion in 1984; the conflict has killed more than 40,000 people and imposed a heavy economic burden on the mainly Kurdish southeast. Analysts see the bill as Ankara's shift toward winding down the conflict through political means — pairing legal guarantees and reintegration pathways for those who disarm with Ocalan's disarmament call — while deliberately avoiding the language of a blanket amnesty, reflecting a careful balance between security concerns and reconciliation (Sources: Al Jazeera; Al Jazeera text).

4. Analysis: from natural disaster to public health and national reconciliation

This hour's international politics runs along three parallel tracks: Colombia's quake thrusts disaster preparedness back onto Latin America's agenda — a president three days into office now faces a national catastrophe, making response capacity and international aid efficiency the first test of his tenure; Trump's vaccine order pushes US public-health policy into direct collision with mainstream medical consensus — with measles cases at a 35-year high, cutting recommended vaccines raises serious public-health concerns, while the federal-state divide leaves the order's enforcement in doubt; and Turkey's PKK bill is a rare "reconciliation signal" on the Middle East-Caucasus map — if the first 3,500 detainees are released as expected, it would be the most substantive progress since Ocalan's 2025 disarmament call.
