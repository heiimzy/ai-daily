Mexico's UNAM Voids Thousands of Exams: Suspected Mass AI Cheating, Experts Estimate ~75,000 Applicants May Have Cheated

According to The New York Times, Mexico's largest public university, the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), has voided thousands of admissions exam results over suspected widespread AI cheating and suspended admissions while it investigates. It was UNAM's first online admissions exam, which required students to be monitored by web cameras.

The scandal erupted in mid-July after test results revealed an unusually high number of perfect or near-perfect scores among applicants for competitive programs such as medicine and law. The number of results annulled so far represents a tiny fraction of the nearly 160,000 exams submitted, but Raúl Rojas, an AI specialist and mathematician at the University of Nevada, Reno, estimates that roughly half the test takers - about 75,000 people - may have cheated in some way. Rojas noted that because the exam was open for a long period, answers leaked and were even sold online, and that many cheaters probably used old-fashioned methods like the cheat sheet rather than AI.

Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum said this week that the university must get to the bottom of the fraud - whether the company contracted to administer the exam, Territorium Life, played a role, or whether individual students used artificial intelligence. Meanwhile, some high-scoring students protested on campus, saying they were being unfairly penalized and that their results could be invalidated simply because of high scores. UNAM President Leonardo Lomelí pledged to "stand on the side of those who studied, who prepared themselves" and to draw lessons for all higher education institutions.

This is hardly the first time AI has been suspected in cheating scandals. Students have been caught using AI-powered smart glasses to cheat on high-stakes exams in England and South Korea; Australian universities had to abandon AI-detection tools after they falsely accused students at alarmingly high rates.

Black Hat USA 2026 Opens: AI Agent Security Is the Dominant Storyline, Trust-Handoff Flaw Found Across Vendors

The world's most prestigious hacking conference, Black Hat USA 2026, opened August 1 at Mandalay Bay in Las Vegas. This year's event features a re-engineered six-day program: hands-on Trainings run August 1-4, Summit Day (including The AI Summit) lands on August 4, and the two-day main Briefings conference takes place August 5-6. AI security sessions hit a record high this year, with agentic AI as the undisputed main theme.

Fifteen research teams opened the conference by disclosing a year's worth of work on breaking AI agents running in production on Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, Microsoft and Salesforce infrastructure. The most structurally significant finding came from Novee Security's Elad Meged: a trust-handoff failure exists across Anthropic, Google and OpenAI pipelines simultaneously - not a single patchable bug but a compositional flaw in how AI pipelines are built. Ori Lahav from Rublik Zero Labs achieved the first documented escape from the Microsoft Copilot sandbox to the host environment: a single document upload triggers Remote Prompt Execution, with blast radius across multiple Azure services.

NVIDIA researchers also fine-tuned a 30B open-source model specifically for agent exploitation, achieving a 56% exploit success rate at 70-125 times lower operating cost; Zenity Labs demonstrated zero-click compromise chains across six major enterprise AI platforms - email-triggered prompt injection can reach a victim's connected Google Drive and inject persistent malicious memories into ChatGPT. Black Hat's full briefing schedule is live on its official website.

Snapchat Stops Recommending Fully AI-Generated Videos: Spotlight Prioritizes Authentic Human Creativity Against "AI Slop"

Snapchat announced on August 1 that it is updating its recommendation system for Spotlight, its short-video feed: wholly AI-generated videos will no longer be eligible for recommendation, even if AI use is disclosed. The company says the goal is to "reward authentic creativity from real people" and keep Spotlight a place to discover original perspectives, personal storytelling, and moments people choose to create and share themselves.

Snapchat is not rejecting AI entirely: content enhanced or edited using Snapchat's own AI creative tools remains eligible for recommendation and will carry visible transparency indicators showing AI played a role. The company noted that unique Spotlight contributors are up more than 120% year over year, which it cites as proof that people are already choosing to create original content.

The move is part of a broader industry crackdown on "AI slop" - low-quality, automated content that floods recommendation feeds. LinkedIn recently rolled out a button letting users flag posts as AI slop, YouTube updated monetization rules to block payouts on generic AI content, and Meta pulled a controversial Instagram AI photo tool after backlash. Snapchat is now the first major social platform to explicitly exclude fully AI-generated videos from its recommendation system.

Y Combinator Open-Sources QM: An MIT-Licensed, Model-Agnostic Multi-Agent Harness Used Across the Company

Y Combinator has open-sourced QM (quartermaster), the multi-agent "harness" it runs internally, under the MIT license, with code published to GitHub (github.com/yc-software/qm). YC uses QM across accounting, legal, events and engineering - including building QM itself - and describes it as a multiplayer agent harness "designed for startups," not a personal assistant.

QM's core design principle is isolation: every employee and every Slack room gets its own scoped memory, files, keychain view, permissions, crons, web apps, and durable sandbox. Technically, QM runs TypeScript on Node with Fastify for HTTP, stores session history and memory in Postgres, and offers Slack as an optional in-process plugin. Crucially, QM is not tied to any single model or tool - Pi, OpenCode, Codex and Claude Code can all drive the same core, so a deployment can swap models, harnesses, session stores and sandboxes without rebuilding around one vendor's roadmap.

YC is candid that QM is "early and has bugs" but has been "surprisingly useful." Its security model follows the pattern of local coding agents: the agent acts as the person it works for, using that person's credentials and permissions, while its actions are audited; security postures range from strict human approval to a "dangerous mode" with no pauses between tool calls. For startups trying to turn AI agents from clever side tools into something closer to company infrastructure, YC publishing both the code and the operational scars of its own operating layer is a rare display of transparency.
