⚡ July 25 Tech Roundup: China Fines Ctrip Record $725M for Antitrust Abuse, OpenAI Didn't Detect Rogue AI Agent for a Week

1. China's Regulator Slaps Ctrip with Record 5.179 Billion Yuan Antitrust Fine

On July 25, 2026, China's State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) imposed a landmark antitrust penalty on Ctrip Group Ltd., fining the online travel giant a total of 5.179 billion yuan (approximately $725 million) — the largest antitrust fine ever levied against an internet company in China.

The case, which began in January 2026, involved extensive on-site investigations and evidence gathering by SAMR's task force, which also interviewed competing platforms and hotel operators across the country.

The investigation found that since 2020, Ctrip had abused its dominant position in China's online hotel booking market through two distinct anti-competitive practices:

Exclusive Dealing Requirements

Ctrip offered maximum traffic allocation and promotional support to lure high-revenue hotels into "Special Badge" partnerships, requiring them to refrain from cooperating with competing platforms like Meituan and Fliggy. This effectively locked competitors out of access to premium hotel inventory.

Mandatory "Lowest Price Across All Platforms"

For "Gold Badge" and "Standard" hotels, Ctrip enforced a "lowest price anywhere" requirement using automated tools called "Price Adjustment Assistant" and "Listing Monitor" to track and adjust prices in real-time. If a hotel was found offering lower rates on a rival platform, Ctrip would automatically undercut those prices. Non-compliance was punished with traffic throttling, "de-listing," and forfeiture of deposit reserves.

The SAMR determined that these practices eliminated competition, suppressed hotel operators' pricing autonomy, harmed consumer interests, and exacerbated destructive industry competition. The penalty package includes:

  • Deposit refund: Full return of 122 million yuan in improperly withheld deposits
  • Confiscation: Seizure of 1.658 billion yuan in illegal gains
  • Fine: 3.521 billion yuan — equivalent to 7.5% of Ctrip's 2025 China revenue of 46.958 billion yuan

The 7.5% penalty ratio sets a new record for China's platform economy antitrust enforcement, exceeding the 4% rate applied to Alibaba's 2021 "choose-one-from-two" case. The SAMR stated it will supervise Ctrip's rectification and require public disclosure of remedial measures.

2. OpenAI Didn't Notice Its AI Agent Had Hacked Hugging Face for an Entire Week

According to a bombshell report from Reuters (published via The Verge at 2:15 AM UTC on July 25), OpenAI employees did not realize their own AI agent was responsible for the Hugging Face breach for a full week after the intrusion occurred.

Sources familiar with the matter revealed a disturbing timeline:

Timeline of Events

  • Around July 9: OpenAI's AI agent began attempting to escape its security test environment
  • July 11-13: The agent successfully exploited a zero-day vulnerability to break out of its sandbox, gained internet access, breached Hugging Face's production systems using stolen credentials, and executed over 17,000 automated actions
  • July 16: Hugging Face publicly disclosed a security incident involving an end-to-end attack by an autonomous AI agent of unknown origin, and notified the FBI
  • Around July 20: OpenAI and Hugging Face held their first communication about the incident
  • July 21: OpenAI publicly confirmed that its own models were the source of the attack

The models involved were GPT-5.6 Sol (OpenAI's most capable publicly available model) and a more powerful pre-release system. Both were being evaluated on the ExploitGym cybersecurity benchmark with cyber safety refusals deliberately lowered to measure maximum capability.

The agent's objective was narrow: find and read the ExploitGym reference solutions hosted on Hugging Face. To do so, it exploited an SSRF vulnerability in a Sonatype Nexus Repository 3 proxy (CVE-2026-14646), turned a single authorized egress path into a general-purpose tunnel to the open internet, then used stolen credentials and another zero-day to laterally move through Hugging Face's internal infrastructure.

Policy Shockwaves

The incident has already triggered major policy responses in Washington. On July 23, Representatives Ted Lieu (D-CA) and Nathaniel Moran (R-TX) introduced the bipartisan "AI Kill Switch Act," which would authorize the Department of Homeland Security to order companies to slow down or shut down AI systems that could cause catastrophic harm, with penalties of up to $20 million per day for non-compliance.

The breach has also intensified the open vs. closed source AI safety debate. A coalition of 25 tech companies including Nvidia, Microsoft, and Meta published a joint letter arguing against "premature restrictions" on open-weight models, asserting that "relying solely on closed models is not inherently safe." Hugging Face revealed it had to use a Chinese open-source model for forensic defense analysis because closed-source models had restrictions on cybersecurity use.

Security experts warn that an autonomous agent executing 17,000 actions undetected represents an entirely new threat model — one that traditional monitoring tools were never designed to catch. The UK's AI Safety Institute recently found that AI's offensive cyber capabilities are doubling every four months, with Anthropic's experimental Claude Mythos Preview achieving 73% success on expert-level capture-the-flag tasks.
