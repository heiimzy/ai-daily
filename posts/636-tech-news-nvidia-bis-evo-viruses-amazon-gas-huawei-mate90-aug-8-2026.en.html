⚡ Aug 8 Tech Roundup: US Commerce Reviews How Chinese AI Firms Get Nvidia Chips Overseas, Evo AI Designs 16 Brand-New Viruses, Amazon Builds Giant Private Gas Plant, Huawei Mate 90 Pro Max Leaks 3D-Stacked Kirin Chip

Over the past hour or two, several major tech storylines accelerated at once: the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) has launched a review into how Chinese AI firms obtain advanced Nvidia chips through legal channels — especially renting overseas compute, with Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 model squarely in its sights; Stanford University and the Arc Institute used the AI model Evo to design 16 brand-new viruses not found in nature, showcasing both the promise and the peril of AI-driven synthetic biology; Amazon confirmed it is funding what could be the largest private gas power plant in US history (7.65GW) to power AI data centers; and the Huawei Mate 90 Pro Max leak reveals a 3D-stacked Kirin 9050 Pro chip and a 7,000mAh battery — a "workaround" strategy for living without EUV lithography. Here is the detailed, timestamp-verified breakdown.

1. US Commerce Reviews How Chinese AI Firms Get Nvidia Chips Overseas: The "Remote Compute" Loophole

According to Bloomberg, the Commerce Department division that typically investigates chip export-control violations is now more systematically examining the legal avenues Chinese AI firms use to access advanced processors — in particular, renting computing power located in other countries to use Nvidia chips. People familiar with the matter say the review follows last month's release of Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 model, which scored nearly as high on benchmarks as the latest offerings from American rivals Anthropic and OpenAI — reigniting the Washington debate over whether export controls are working.

A senior White House official had earlier posted on social media accusing Moonshot of illegally acquiring Nvidia's best hardware and remotely accessing those same chips through an unnamed party in Thailand. Insiders say that post was not coordinated with the Commerce Department, but a few days later BIS's enforcement arm launched this review. The effort involves compiling two lists: countries with alleged black-market operations moving restricted Nvidia chips physically into China, and countries where Chinese firms access the chips remotely — the latter normally falls outside traditional export-control territory, since remote access itself is not illegal.

A typical structure under scrutiny: Alibaba accesses Nvidia chips located in Malaysia through Megaspeed International, a Singaporean firm under US investigation for possible chip diversion — but the agreement is not directly between the two companies; instead, Alibaba used a Singaporean shell controlled by a Cayman Islands entity, of which the Chinese tech giant is the ultimate holding company. Whether BIS has legal authority to restrict cloud-computing agreements remains contested — the US House has passed bipartisan legislation to establish that power, but any effort to curb Chinese firms' use of overseas data centers would almost certainly face opposition from Nvidia and other tech companies. Nvidia has previously slammed export controls for making it "forfeit the world's second-largest commercial market to foreign competitors."

2. AI Designs 16 Brand-New Viruses: Stanford and the Arc Institute's Evo Model

In a study published this week, scientists used an AI program called Evo to design several novel viruses not found in nature — the team stresses the viruses currently only infect E. coli and pose no threat to humans, but the "breakthrough" shows both AI's potential in synthetic biology and raises fears about weaponization. Morning Brew and other outlets covered the milestone as "AI created 16 brand-new viruses."

Here's how it works: Evo was trained on over 9 trillion nucleotides — the building blocks of DNA and RNA — to spot patterns humans have yet to recognize. After digesting nature's code, the program spat out about 700,000 potential genome combinations. Scientists at Stanford and the Arc Institute tested 285 of the recipes they deemed viable — and 16 of them grew real-life viruses that have never been produced before.

Not all viruses are bad, the researchers note: doctors have long used engineered viruses to fight drug-resistant bacteria and genetic diseases, and AI could make that work easier. But the risks are real — a bad actor, or a scientist without adequate guardrails, could use the technology to design a bioweapon. The researchers explicitly warned that this kind of study should not be applied to dangerous pathogens that can infect humans, such as malaria. The case is the latest illustration of the AI double-edged sword: the same technology that can save lives can also be abused.

3. Amazon Confirms Giant Private Gas Plant: 7.65GW to Power AI Data Centers

AFP reports that Amazon confirmed Friday it is financially backing a massive private gas power plant in Texas that could become the single largest source of greenhouse gas emissions in the United States. Market-intelligence firm Cleanview, which reviewed satellite imagery to connect three data-center construction permits Amazon filed this week to the plant, first reported the news. The facility, known as GW Ranch, is being developed by Pacifico Energy.

Previously filed permits show the plant would have 35 turbines and generate 7.65 gigawatts — larger than any gas plant currently operating in the United States; located in Pecos County, Texas, it would be permitted to emit more than 30 million metric tonnes of greenhouse gases a year, more than any other single source in the country (though facilities rarely emit at their permitted ceiling). Separately, an even bigger 9.2GW facility is planned in Ohio under a public-private partnership involving SoftBank, connecting to the grid as well as powering a data center.

Amazon's official line: by generating its own power, it avoids passing the cost on to ordinary Americans, whose electricity bills have spiked as utilities charge consumers for the new infrastructure feeding the AI boom. "Amazon believes in paying the full costs of powering our operations," a spokesperson said. "Our new planned data center campus in Pecos County does just that: it's powered by new on-site generation that won't raise electricity costs for Texas families." The company added it is exploring on-site solar and battery storage, remains committed to net-zero carbon by 2040 under The Climate Pledge, and has enabled 40 carbon-free energy projects totaling nearly 10GW in Texas.

The move marks "behind-the-meter" power going mainstream: in 2024 Elon Musk's xAI deployed mobile gas turbines to its data center in Memphis, and now every hyperscaler is following the "off-grid, self-built power" playbook to get AI compute online faster.

4. Huawei Mate 90 Pro Max Leak: 3D-Stacked Kirin 9050 Pro, Working Around Lithography Limits

Tech outlet Memeburn reports that leaked specs for the Huawei Mate 90 Pro Max show a "LogicFolding" Kirin 9050 Pro chip: instead of chasing smaller transistors, Huawei folds and stacks logic circuits across two layers bonded together — "like building a two-story house instead of a bigger single-story one." US sanctions block Huawei from buying EUV lithography machines (the ultra-precise tools needed for sub-5nm chips), so the company is breaking through vertically in packaging architecture instead.

According to the leaked data, the Kirin 9050 Pro delivers 53.5% more transistor density, 37.5% smaller chip area, and 41% lower power consumption versus last year's Kirin 9030 Pro. He Tingbo, Huawei's semiconductor chief, unveiled LogicFolding at the IEEE International Symposium on Circuits and Systems in Shanghai this past May, revealing that Huawei has quietly designed and mass-produced 381 chips using the framework over six years — the Kirin 9050 Pro is simply the first to go commercial.

The rest of the spec sheet is equally aggressive: a 6,800-7,000mAh battery (up from the Mate 80 Pro Max's 6,000mAh), 100W wired fast charging, WiFi 7, a 6.9-inch flat 1.5K LTPO dual-layer OLED display, and a 200MP main camera. On software, it runs HarmonyOS 7, which entered developer beta in June — meaning no Google Play, no Gmail, no YouTube out of the box, still the single biggest barrier for buyers outside China, though the ecosystem has matured considerably for domestic users. If the leak holds, the phone will stand as a landmark case of "sanctions forcing architectural innovation."

5. What to Watch

Over the next 24-48 hours, watch for: whether the BIS review of "remote compute rental" turns into new policy or enforcement action, and how lobbying by Nvidia and others shapes it; whether regulatory discussion triggered by Evo's synthetic viruses leads to new AI biosafety rules; actual construction progress on Amazon's GW Ranch plant and follow-ups by other cloud providers; and the official launch cadence for the Huawei Mate 90 Pro Max, plus whether 3D-stacked chips get adopted more widely. Taken together, chips, energy, and AI are the three interwoven threads defining the broader tech picture this August.
