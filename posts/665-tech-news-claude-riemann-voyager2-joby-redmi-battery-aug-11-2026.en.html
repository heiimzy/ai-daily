1. Anthropic: Unreleased Research Claude Makes Major Progress on the Riemann Hypothesis

According to IT Home (published Aug 11, 18:35 CST), Anthropic announced that an unreleased research version of Claude has made significant progress toward the Riemann hypothesis. In a multi-day, model-driven test, Claude raised the long-standing lower bound on the proportion of zeros of the Riemann zeta function lying on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. The work was done entirely in Claude Code: the two test runs generated 31 million output tokens, and after 650 failed attempts the model marshaled 60 sub-agents, executing 2,400 shell commands and writing hundreds of Python scripts.

Anthropic then had the results checked by in-house mathematicians Levent Alpoge and Ralph Furman, with external number theorists Brian Conrey and Dan Goldston reviewing the work; employee Eric Easley also helped turn the proof into a form verifiable in Lean. On method: Claude constructed a suitable function space and introduced a Weil-induced quadratic form—zeros on the critical line form a positive-definite subspace, zeros off the line a negative-definite one—then used first- and second-moment information to establish a rank inequality for the quadratic form. The key step was treating the entire space uniformly while allowing non-diagonal forms. First proposed in 1859, the Riemann hypothesis carries a $1 million prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute. Anthropic published Claude's paper, expert commentary, process logs and a Lean-formalized proof, but did not say whether the multi-agent capability would be opened to the public soon. US tech outlet The Verge (Aug 11, 11:00 UTC) covered the news under the headline "The AI takeover of mathematics has begun."

2. NASA's 'Big Bang' Update Gives Voyager 2 One More Year

According to Engadget (published Aug 11, 7:58 am EST, by Mariella Moon), NASA recently changed Voyager 2's settings in an operation it called "Big Bang," allowing the probe—almost 50 years into its mission—to keep running and doing science in its current state for one more year. Voyager 2's power comes from a radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG) that converts heat from decaying plutonium into electricity; as the plutonium depletes, the probe loses about 4 watts of power every year. It launched in 1977 with 10 functional scientific instruments; only three remain as NASA deactivates them to preserve energy, and the agency would have had to shut down one more later this year without this tweak.

"Big Bang" involved turning off certain powered devices simultaneously and replacing them with lower-power alternatives, while ensuring the spacecraft would stay warm enough to operate. Voyager 2 launched about two weeks before its twin, Voyager 1, reached Jupiter in 1979 and visited all the gas giants within the decade. In 2018 it entered interstellar space, and it has been sending back data on the density and temperature of plasma outside the heliosphere ever since.

3. Joby Aviation Acquires Resonant Sciences for $500 Million, Pushing into Defense

According to The Verge (published Aug 11, 12:00 PM UTC, by Andrew J. Hawkins), eVTOL maker Joby Aviation announced it is acquiring Resonant Sciences, a Dayton, Ohio-based defense firm, in a $500 million deal as it looks to expand further into the military industrial complex. Joby says it expects to finance the deal with $450 million in cash and $50 million in equity; closing is expected in early 2027. After the deal closes, Resonant will continue its existing work as Joby's dedicated defense division, while the commercial division stays focused on air taxis—the first of which are expected to launch later this year.

The deal is the latest example of the fledgling electric aviation industry looking beyond urban air taxis for new revenue streams, especially as rising costs and a protracted regulatory approval process push air taxi success further into the future. With several eVTOL players already pivoting toward defense contracts, Joby's move underscores the broader industry trend.

5. CAS Dalian Team's Lithium Primary Battery: 'Dual-High' Breakthrough in Endurance and Power

According to IT Home (published Aug 11, 18:53 CST, citing Science and Technology Daily), a team led by researcher Chen Zhongwei at the Dalian Institute of Chemical Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences has developed a 25-Ah soft-pack lithium metal primary battery. Tested by the safety laboratory of the China Electronics Standardization Institute, the battery delivers more than 750Wh/kg and 1,285Wh/L at low discharge rates—among the highest energy densities for primary batteries—while raising the discharge rate up to 100-fold, achieving 10C short-duration high-current discharge, putting China at the international forefront of high-power primary batteries.

Traditional lithium primary batteries can store power for years but struggle in moments of peak demand: a drone caught in turbulence needs a sudden hover correction, a robot must burst over rough terrain, a spacecraft's attitude control demands seconds of high current, and a field seismometer must be "awake and ready" after years in the wild. The new battery holds 656Wh/kg and 1,130Wh/L at 1C discharge, delivers a specific power of 4,487W/kg at room temperature, sustains 4C continuous discharge, retains 447Wh/kg at 1C in −40°C cold, and self-discharges under 1% per year. Starting from the cathode, the team designed a high-entropy oxide with a specific capacity above 430mAh/g and built a 10-ton-per-year pilot line, covering the whole chain from materials to cells. The battery has passed third-party certification and is being validated in drones, robots, spacecraft simulators and field sensor nodes.
