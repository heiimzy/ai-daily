⚡ August 5 Tech Update: OpenAI Fires Back at Apple's Trade-Secret Lawsuit, SpaceX Posts First Earnings Since IPO With $18.4B Capex, Arista Guides Higher on AI Networking, Valar Atomics Raises $1B for Microreactors

Four stories dominated the tech world over the past few hours: OpenAI unusually published employee chat logs to fight Apple's trade-secret lawsuit; SpaceX delivered its first earnings report since its IPO — beating on revenue but getting dragged down by $18.37 billion in capital spending; Arista Networks guided well above consensus on surging AI-driven networking demand; and nuclear startup Valar Atomics closed a massive $1 billion funding round. Here is this round's tech roundup.

1. OpenAI publicly hits back at Apple's lawsuit, publishing employee chats and calling out 'residual access' blame-shifting

According to SiliconANGLE (August 4, 2026, 23:01 UTC), OpenAI Group PBC pushed back publicly against the trade-secret lawsuit Apple filed last month, outlining its arguments in a blog post published Monday. The move came shortly after Apple submitted a new court filing asking for expedited evidence collection and a preliminary injunction barring OpenAI from accessing, using or disclosing the allegedly stolen intellectual property. In its post, OpenAI took aim at several core arguments and — notably — published correspondence between its employees and Apple staffers, saying the chat logs and emails weaken the iPhone maker's case.

The suit centers on former Apple engineer Chang Liu, who Apple says failed to return a company laptop before leaving for OpenAI and downloaded dozens of internal files with sensitive product information using a "rare, previously unknown authentication bug." OpenAI countered that Apple "now tries to shift the blame to 'residual access'" — permissions left active by accident — and called such permissions a common issue at Apple. The lawsuit also names Tang Tan, Apple's former vice president of product design who joined OpenAI last year, over allegations that he asked former Apple staffers to bring internal component prototypes to job interviews; OpenAI pushed back, noting that "Tang served Apple for more than 24 years and was widely known as one of the most innovative leaders at the company."

Background: Apple filed suit last month accusing OpenAI of stealing intellectual property to support its push into consumer electronics — OpenAI reportedly plans a line of AI-powered devices to compete with the iPhone. How the litigation will affect that hardware push remains unclear.

2. SpaceX's first earnings since IPO: revenue up 92% beats estimates, but $18.4B capex sends stock down more than 8%

According to SiliconANGLE (August 4, 2026, 22:36 UTC / 18:36 EDT), SpaceX reported its first quarterly results since its June initial public offering — the largest on record. For the quarter ended June 30, the company posted a net loss of 9 cents per share on revenue of $7.81 billion (up 92% from $4.07 billion a year earlier), beating analyst expectations of a 26-cent loss on $6.93 billion in revenue. Adjusted EBITDA rose 191% to $3.54 billion. The number that moved the stock, however, was capex: SpaceX spent $18.37 billion in the quarter — more than six times the $2.83 billion a year earlier and well ahead of the roughly $13 billion analysts had penciled in — of which $15.83 billion came from the AI segment built on the xAI business absorbed in February.

By segment, the fastest growth came from AI: revenue jumped 247% to $2.56 billion, cloud services agreements signed during the quarter carry $14.1 billion in contracted sales, and compute capacity reached 1.4 gigawatts, up from 0.4 gigawatts a year earlier, as work continued on the Colossus II data center. The segment still lost $1.26 billion at the operating line, though adjusted EBITDA turned positive at $1.15 billion. Connectivity — home to Starlink — remains the profit engine: revenue climbed 66% to $4.29 billion and operating income rose 79% to $1.66 billion, with Starlink ending June at 12 million subscribers, double the count a year earlier. Enterprise and government sales more than doubled to $1.81 billion, and Starshield picked up more than $6 billion in multiyear US contracts. SpaceX also agreed during the quarter to acquire Cursor maker Anysphere for $60 billion, expected to close in Q3, and shipped Grok 4.5 in July. The company gave no guidance; shares fell more than 8% in late trading.

3. Arista guides Q3 revenue to $3.3B, far above consensus, as AI networking demand stays hot

According to Reuters (August 4, 2026, 22:14 UTC), Arista Networks forecast third-quarter revenue of $3.3 billion on Tuesday — well above analysts' average estimate of $2.94 billion (LSEG data) — and shares rose about 3% after the bell. The company also beat second-quarter expectations, posting revenue of $3.04 billion and adjusted EPS of $1.02, versus estimates of $2.82 billion and 88 cents. It guided third-quarter adjusted EPS to $1.06-$1.08, also above the 91-cent consensus.

Arista makes the high-speed networking gear that powers big data centers for customers like Microsoft and Amazon, and has been expanding beyond its core cloud customer base into enterprise campus and branch networking to diversify revenue. The strong guidance is the latest sign that demand for high-speed switches is accelerating as companies race to build out AI infrastructure.

4. Nuclear microreactor startup Valar Atomics raises $1B led by Sequoia to commercialize its helium-cooled Ward 250

According to Canary Media (August 4, 2026, 22:00 UTC / 18:00 EDT), Torrance, California-based Valar Atomics announced Monday its latest funding round, led by venture capital giant Sequoia, raising $1 billion to lay the groundwork for commercializing Valar's 5-megawatt Ward 250 reactor, which uses helium as a coolant instead of water. The reactor design has yet to receive regulatory approval.

Founder and CEO Isaiah Taylor said the new Series B financing lets Valar "transition from demonstrating the operability of an integrated reactor system to producing fleets of them en masse," pursuing a fully "vertically integrated, hardware first approach" covering reactor deployment, long-term operations and fuel production. The company — once derided within the nuclear industry over its 27-year-old founder's audacious social media claims — is now a favorite of the Trump administration: last August it was picked for a Department of Energy pilot program, and in February it made history by airlifting Ward 250 parts on a C-17 Globemaster III from California to Utah's Hill Air Force Base in what the White House called a "groundbreaking" first demonstration.

The hurdles remain steep: the US operated a high-temperature gas-cooled reactor in the 1970s (Colorado's Fort St. Vrain) but shut it down after a decade of costly maintenance issues; China started building its first major high-temperature gas-cooled reactor in 2012, split atoms for the first time in 2021, and reignited the commercialization race in December 2023. With Europe's heat wave forcing inland water-cooled nuclear plants to cut production, reactors that don't depend on water for cooling look especially promising — but microreactor economics still need to be proven.
