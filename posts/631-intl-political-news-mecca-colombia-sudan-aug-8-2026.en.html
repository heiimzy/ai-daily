⚡ Aug 8 World Politics: Saudi-Turkey-Pakistan Sign the Mecca Joint Defence Pact, Colombia's Right-wing Newcomer Sworn In Vowing to Crush Cartels, UN Warns Sudan's War Is Robbing an Entire Generation of Its Future

In the past hour or two, major geopolitical moves have landed in the Middle East, Latin America and Africa at once, all circling one question: who will write the post-war order. Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement in the holy city of Mecca, stipulating that an armed attack on any one party shall be regarded as an attack on all three — read by analysts as a landmark signal that the regional order is being reshaped by the US-Israeli war on Iran. At the same time, Colombia's right-wing newcomer Abelardo De La Espriella was sworn in as president in Cali, vowing to "relentlessly crush narco-terrorism" and abandon the dialogue route with armed groups. And the United Nations has delivered its starkest warning yet on the 1,210-day Sudan war, which has left nearly three-quarters of schools damaged and five in six children out of school — robbing "an entire life of its future."

1. The Mecca Joint Defence Agreement: three states define a "collective attack"

On Friday, Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif formally signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement in Mecca. The joint statement said the agreement is intended "to strengthen collective deterrence against any act of aggression," stipulating that "any armed attack against any one of the three states shall be regarded as an attack against them all," while also "providing a framework for exchanges across all levels of defence cooperation." Compared with the "imminent" pact reported when Erdogan visited Jeddah a week earlier, this formal signing extends the original Saudi-Pakistan Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement of September 2025 into a tripartite framework.

Analysts widely read the move as a signal that regional powers, shaken in their confidence in US security commitments, are hedging their bets. Kristian Coates Ulrichsen, a Middle East fellow at Rice University's Baker Institute, told Al Jazeera that regional states have harboured "growing doubts about the reliability of the US security partnership for more than a decade," and that "the decision to open the war on Iran, the course of the war and its on-and-off diplomacy have only deepened those concerns and accelerated diversification in security and defence." He stressed the pact is not meant to "replace or displace the US," but "to offer more strategic and policy options."

Washington's silence is telling: as of publication, the administration has not formally commented. Sina Toossi of the Center for International Policy was blunter — calling it "one of the first major geopolitical signs that the regional order is being reshaped by the US war on Iran," its significance "less in the formal military alliance itself than in what it says about how regional states are reassessing their own security." The signing came as Iran-Oman talks on the Strait of Hormuz agreement were reportedly in their "final stage," pushing the region's security reset even faster.

2. Colombia's "Tiger" takes office: abandoning dialogue, vowing to crush cartels

Latin America's rightward shift deepens. Colombia's right-wing newcomer Abelardo De La Espriella sworn in as president in Cali — by choice of venue rather than the capital Bogota, itself a powerful gesture: Cali sits in the volatile southwest long scarred by conflict with armed groups. Known as "El Tigre" (The Tiger), De La Espspriella is a self-made lawyer and businessman who calls himself an outsider and largely self-financed, defeating leftist incumbent Gustavo Petro in June's runoff.

In his address he branded Petro's negotiated approach a "failure," vowed "no room for that undermine the stability of the nation," and pledged to "relentlessly crush narco-terrorism," declaring "the option of dialogue is completely exhausted." He has pledged to join the US-backed "Shield of the Americas" regional anti-narcotics coalition, restore ties with Israel, and cut ties with Cuba and Venezuela — a clear turn toward Trump.

Security dominated the ceremony: soldiers in full gear and armoured vehicles sealed the roads to the auditorium, with police carrying out explosives screening for days beforehand. After the ceremony he went straight to a nearby army battalion to address troops, seeking to bolster morale — the southwest still hosts ELN, the Clan del Golfo cartel and FARC dissidents. Observers expect a large-scale military sweep against them.

3. UN warns: Sudan's war is robbing "an entire generation of its future"

At an informal Security Council meeting on safeguarding education in Sudan on Friday, UN Deputy Secretary-General Aminar Mohammed delivered a heavy warning: this war, now in its 1,210th day, is "robbing an entire generation of its future." She noted that since the conflict began between Sudan's armed forces and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in April 2023, nearly three-quarters of schools in Darfur have been damaged or made unusable, with "five out of six children out of school." Parents would rather keep children home, she said, because "home is the only place left where they can keep them safe."

The figures are stark: since 2024 the UN has documented 67 attacks on schools and 154 instances of schools being used for military purposes, with the true toll "almost certainly understated." More than half of Sudan's teachers have gone unpaid, yet many keep teaching "against all odds." The UN and partners have brought more than a million Sudanese children back into some form of learning this year, but it remains far short of need. "Sudan has already lost a generation in its earlier wars, and it is losing another one now," Mohammed said, while the heaviest burden falls on women and children. Some 15 million people are now internally displaced across the country.
