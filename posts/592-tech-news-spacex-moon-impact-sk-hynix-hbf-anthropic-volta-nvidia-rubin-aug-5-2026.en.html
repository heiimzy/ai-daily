⚡ August 5 Tech Update: SpaceX Falcon 9 Stage to Slam Into Moon Today, SK hynix Unveils HBF Standard and V10 375-Layer NAND, Anthropic Signs $10B Compute Deal, Nvidia Reviews Downscaling Rubin Ultra HBM

Wednesday morning (August 5) has already brought a packed docket for the global tech scene: a spent SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage that has drifted in space for 18 months is set to slam into the Moon today at roughly 06:35 UTC, carving out an artificial crater about 27 meters wide; in Silicon Valley, SK hynix and SanDisk used FMS 2026 to unveil the industry's first High Bandwidth Flash (HBF) standard spec and publicly showcase the world's first V10 375-layer 4D NAND; the AI compute arms race keeps accelerating as Anthropic is reported to have signed a $10 billion compute deal with Nvidia-backed cloud startup Volta; and TrendForce warns DRAM supply stays tight through 2027, forcing Nvidia to review downscaling the HBM configuration of next-gen Rubin Ultra. Here is this round's general tech roundup.

1. SpaceX Falcon 9 Upper Stage to Hit the Moon Today: ~3 Tons of TNT, a ~27m Crater

According to Space.com, SpaceNews and CBC, a spent SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage (catalog designation 2025-010D) is expected to strike the lunar surface at about 2:35 a.m. ET on August 5 (06:35 UTC / 14:35 Beijing time), traveling at roughly 2.43 km/s (about 8,700 km/h, ~7 times the speed of sound), with the impact point near Einstein Crater.

The ~12-meter, ~3,900-kg stage comes from the January 15, 2025 lunar launch that carried Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost-1 lander and Japan's ispace Hakuto-R "Resilience" lander. Because high-energy lunar trajectories do not allow the controlled deorbit used for low-Earth-orbit missions, the spent stage was left abandoned in a moon-crossing highly elliptical orbit. Independent astronomer Bill Gray, creator of the Project Pluto software, tracked it from around September 2025 and eventually determined it was on a collision course with the Moon.

Scientists estimate the impact will pack the energy of about three tons of TNT, producing a brief flash, kicking up more than a million kilograms of lunar debris, and leaving a crater roughly 20-30 meters wide and about 5 meters deep. Because the strike occurs on the partly illuminated near-side western limb, South America and the low-to-mid latitudes of North America offer the best ground observation. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) and South Korea's Danuri orbiter will image the site before and after to compare. Meanwhile SpaceX has confirmed it is working with NASA to study better ways to dispose of upper stages on high-energy lunar missions, so that space junk does not endanger increasingly busy lunar traffic and future lunar bases.

2. SK hynix and SanDisk Unveil First HBF Standard Spec, Showcase World-First V10 375-Layer 4D NAND

According to the SK hynix Newsroom and the SanDisk announcement (August 3, 2026), SK hynix, together with SanDisk, unveiled the industry's first High Bandwidth Flash (HBF) standard specification at FMS 2026 (Future of Memory and Storage, Aug 4-6) in Santa Clara, California, releasing it as an open standard through the Open Compute Project (OCP) framework.

Positioned as a new memory tier between HBM and SSD, HBF is designed to break the "memory wall" in the AI era. The first spec covers two configurations based on 8-high and 16-high NAND stacks, capacities up to 512GB, and three bandwidth grades delivering roughly 0.4TB/s to 3.0TB/s, adopting the UCIe universal chiplet interface so it can flexibly connect with GPUs and CPUs of different architectures. Notably, Google and AI chip startup Tenstorrent have joined the HBF consortium, providing technical validation — underscoring that HBF targets the real needs of hyperscale data centers in the AI inference era from day one.

At the same event, SK hynix also publicly showcased its tenth-generation (V10) 375-layer 4D NAND wafer and products for the first time — currently the highest-layer-count NAND in the world. Compared with the prior generation, V10 improves performance per watt by about 2.5 times, ideal for power-hungry data-center workloads. The company plans to bring high-performance, high-capacity enterprise SSDs (eSSDs) based on 375-layer NAND into mass production in early 2027, completing what it calls the industry's only full-stack AI memory portfolio spanning DRAM (HBM), the intermediate HBF tier, and storage (eSSD).

3. Anthropic Signs $10B Compute Deal With Nvidia-Backed Cloud Startup Volta: Six-Year Norway Data Center Deal

According to Bloomberg, TheNextWeb and Reuters (August 4, 2026), AI developer Anthropic has signed a $10 billion, six-year computing capacity agreement with Nvidia-backed cloud infrastructure startup Volta Infra Holdings, locking in capacity at a data center in Norway.

The Norway facility offers about 133 MW of capacity, will be operated by Bitcoin miner Bitdeer, and is to be stocked with Nvidia's latest Vera Rubin chips. Volta, founded in January by former Brookfield Asset Management executives, recently closed a $300M venture round at a $2.4B valuation and secured $5 billion in financing capacity from Azora. Volta disclosed the deal the same day without naming the client; Anthropic and Bitdeer both declined to comment.

The deal — betting compute capacity on a startup that is only months old — reflects Anthropic's seemingly insatiable appetite for compute. It has already inked compute agreements with SpaceX, AMD and Akamai, struck its largest compute deal yet with Google and Broadcom at a roughly $30 billion run rate, and is reportedly in talks to lease capacity from Meta's data centers. Volta's rise also underscores a broader "AI infrastructure financialization" trend: chipmakers, clouds and financiers are increasingly funding the very customers who buy from them. That accelerates AI deployment but also raises concerns about magnified losses if AI demand disappoints. Anthropic earlier this year raised $65 billion and is reportedly weighing a public-market debut as soon as this year.

4. TrendForce: DRAM Stays in Short Supply Through 2027, Nvidia Reviews Cutting Rubin Ultra's HBM Configuration

According to TrendForce (August 4, 2026), with DRAM demand outstripping supply expected to persist through 2027 and validation progress on suppliers' HBM4e uncertain, Nvidia has, since Q3 2026, shifted the HBM configuration for its next-gen Rubin Ultra from the baseline HBM4e 12-high design to evaluating multiple alternatives in parallel — HBM4e 8-high, HBM4 12-high, and HBM4 8-high — with no final decision made yet.

TrendForce attributes the shift to two supply-side bottlenecks: first, overall DRAM supply staying tight through 2027 will limit the wafer capacity suppliers can allocate to HBM; second, the validation and yield-ramp of HBM4e 12-high remain uncertain. Beyond Nvidia, some cloud service providers (CSPs) are also considering trimming the HBM capacity of their next-gen in-house ASICs, as supply-chain risk ripples from the memory sector up into AI chip design.

TrendForce also warns that HBM shipments in 2027, while expected to grow 50%-60% year over year, will still fall short of soaring demand. In a supply-short market, pricing power in 2027 HBM negotiations is expected to stay with suppliers, and a significant rise in HBM unit prices has become an industry consensus. Against this backdrop, AI chipmakers face the twin pressures of tighter HBM supply and higher procurement costs — strengthening the incentive to adopt lower-capacity HBM, which means the next-gen AI hardware "memory arms race" may be forced to strike a more pragmatic balance between performance and supply certainty.
