⚡ Aug 3 Tech Update: Global 'RAMaggeddon' Memory Crunch, Samsung's Bid to Dethrone SK hynix in HBM, CXMT Nears First LPDDR6

The AI compute arms race is delivering an unwelcome surprise to everyday consumers: a global shortage of standard memory chips. Dubbed "RAMaggeddon," the crunch comes as chipmakers pivot their production lines toward the far more profitable high-bandwidth memory (HBM) used to train and run AI models, squeezing DRAM supply for ordinary PCs and smartphones. Meanwhile, a fierce battle is heating up among the world's three memory giants over the next-generation HBM4 and LPDDR6 standards — can Samsung reclaim the crown, and can China's CXMT use DUV lithography to break through Western technology barriers? Here's the full picture of this memory-chip upheaval.

'RAMaggeddon': An AI-Driven Global Memory Price Surge

In the face of the AI dividend, memory chipmakers have taken a rather "practical" stance: while profit-rich HBM is in short supply, standard memory is squeezed. At a shop in Hong Kong's Wan Chai Computer Center, printouts about the memory shortage are pinned beside a price list to explain to customers "what happened." According to Agence France-Presse (AFP), a 16GB RAM stick that used to cost HK$300-400 now sells for about HK$1,500.

"When prices suddenly get this expensive, customers hit a psychological barrier." — Ken Tam, manager of Videocom Computer, Hong Kong

Ellie Wang, an analyst at Taiwan-based market research firm TrendForce, said memory prices for PCs and smartphones are up about five to six times versus a year ago. The surge is spilling into end products: rising laptop and smartphone prices are just the beginning, as automakers report climbing costs for in-vehicle computing systems, and new-vehicle price increases could follow soon.

The crunch even has an official-sounding nickname. Samsung Electronics' chief financial officer said this week that the shortage of memory microchips will likely deepen in 2027 and stay tight through 2028. As memory becomes core production material for AI training and inference, the tight supply is expected to persist throughout the expansion phase of the AI cycle.

The HBM4 Crown: UBS Sees Samsung Dethroning SK hynix

A more technical — and more consequential — battle is unfolding over high-bandwidth memory (HBM). According to a UBS report published Sunday, cited by The Korea Herald, UBS projects Samsung will capture 41% of global HBM bit shipments in 2027, narrowly ahead of SK hynix's 39%, with Micron Technology holding 20%. This is an acceleration from UBS's May forecast, which had only predicted Samsung would reach parity with SK hynix by next year.

The prediction points to Samsung regaining the No.1 spot in the HBM4 generation. In its second-quarter earnings call Thursday, Samsung said HBM4 revenue in the third quarter will more than triple from the previous quarter, and is expected to account for more than 60% of total HBM revenue in the second half. Counterpoint Research data shows Samsung led the global DRAM market in the first quarter with a 38% share, followed by SK hynix at 29%.

SK hynix, of course, is fighting back. In its Wednesday earnings call, the company said it began mass-producing and supplying HBM4 to key customers in the second quarter, with yields and product quality approaching its mature HBM3E products. UBS estimates SK hynix's HBM capacity will rise from 230,000 wafers per month by end of this year to 270,000 by end of next year. Whoever secures certification and stable orders from hyperscalers like Nvidia will lock in the next wave of AI infrastructure gains.

CXMT's First LPDDR6: The Ceiling of DUV Lithography

On the mobile side, China's ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) is catching up at a breathtaking pace. According to a TechTimes report dated August 2, industry sources confirmed that CXMT's hefei-based first LPDDR6 chip, designed to run at 12,800 Mbps (12.8 Gbps), is nearing the end of "development validation" — the second of four production milestones. That puts CXMT past the halfway point toward mass production.

But two numbers reveal the deeper lithography chasm behind this race: 12.8 Gbps versus 14.4 Gbps. CXMT's first LPDDR6 hits the former; SK hynix's EUV-based 1c-process LPDDR6 targets the latter — the practical ceiling of the JEDEC JESD209-6 standard, also aligned with Cadence's confirmed 14.4 Gbps. This ~11% gap is not a "version-two-vs-later-revision" difference; it is the DUV (deep-ultraviolet) lithography constraint showing up directly in mobile memory specs for the first time.

"CXMT is not choosing a slower speed on purpose — it is targeting the speed the DUV process can reliably yield at acceptable cost." — TechTimes analysis

CXMT's G4 process node is built entirely on DUV multi-patterning: 193-nanometer light runs each layer through two to four exposure passes. That works at LPDDR5X speeds, but at LPDDR6's narrower signal-timing windows, accumulated overlay errors likely force speed binning — some dies are graded at 12.8 Gbps instead of the design target. ASML has never shipped a single EUV machine to any Chinese customer, and Dutch export controls maintained under US pressure since 2019 prohibit it. This is a physical barrier CXMT cannot cross in the near term.

Even so, CXMT's engine remains ferocious. The company poured about ¥9.593 billion (roughly $1.42 billion) into R&D in 2025, up 51% year-over-year, with cumulative R&D from 2023 to 2025 exceeding ¥20.6 billion. In the first quarter of 2026, CXMT revenue hit ¥50.8 billion (about $7.52 billion), a 719% year-over-year surge, filling the commodity DRAM gap left by Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron when those companies redirected wafer capacity toward HBM for AI servers.

From Phones to Cars: The Memory 'Arms Race' of the AI Era

The deeper logic of this semiconductor energy transfer is AI's insatiable hunger for memory bandwidth. LPDDR6, standardized by JEDEC as JESD209-6 in July 2025, is one key enabler of on-device AI: SK hynix's 14.4 Gbps LPDDR6 chip delivers up to 38.4 GB/s per die — about 2.25x the effective throughput of the fastest LPDDR5X. SK hynix has confirmed mass-production readiness on its 1c process, with Xiaomi widely reported as the first customer, and OPPO and Vivo reportedly next.

For enterprises, the HBM-versus-DRAM trade-off means AI data-center compute costs stay elevated, while ordinary consumers foot the bill for the whole boom — more expensive RAM, pricier PCs, and phones, with cars following. As Charles Brousse, a graphic designer and custom PC builder, told AFP: "The fact it is driven by the speculative bubble of AI is frustrating, because I'm a graphic designer by trade, so AI has a real impact on my profession."

Three narratives define today's memory-chip landscape — price surges, the HBM4 dynasty change, and CXMT's march toward the frontier. In the AI era, memory chips are being upgraded from mere "components" to "strategic resources". Who controls enough memory bandwidth will hold real leverage over the AI revolution.
